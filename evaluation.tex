\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Settings and Details}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and metrics.}
Our primary benchmark is video object detection on the ILSVRC 2015
ImageNet VID dataset, chosen because dense spatial prediction is the
hardest case for token substitution and therefore the most informative
stress test of persistent reuse. We report mean average precision at IoU
$0.5$ (mAP@50) on the full 639-video validation split, following the
protocol of prior work \cite{eventful,maskvd}. Two companion evaluations
broaden the scope along the axes this benchmark leaves fixed: video
action recognition with ViViT on Kinetics-400, where the prediction is
read globally from a class token rather than per location (Top-1 and
Top-5 accuracy on the full 19{,}877-clip validation split, reported in
the action-recognition subsection below), and ViTDet-B at
$1024 \times 1024$ input resolution. \textbf{[TBD: ViTDet-B
$1024 \times 1024$ results.]} Compute is reported in GFLOPs measured with in-code
operation counters (instrumented linear, matmul, einsum, bias and add
modules) rather than analytic estimates: every reported reduction reflects
operations actually executed, including the cost of PSM's own
matching. Latency is CUDA-event wall-clock time per frame and memory is
peak GPU allocation. Both are measured on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000
(24\,GB) under a warm-up and reset protocol applied identically to every
method.

\paragraph{Ablation split and hyperparameter selection.}
Ablations use a fixed $10\%$ subsample of each dataset: 64 of the 639 VID
videos and 1{,}988 of the 19{,}877 Kinetics-400 clips. Each subsample is
drawn once and shared by every configuration, so all ablation comparisons
are paired. Both subsamples come from their validation splits, so each is
contained in, not disjoint from, the set on which headline numbers are
reported; neither benchmark provides a third partition for tuning under
the standard protocol, and prior work tunes on the same splits
\cite{eventful,maskvd}. The operating points carried into the full-split
comparisons ($\gamma = 0.25$, $T = 6$ for detection; $\gamma = 0.95$,
$T = 2$ for action recognition) were fixed on the subsamples before the
full-split runs; no configuration was selected on the full split. We note
the residual selection bias this implies and observe that it is shared by
every compared method's published protocol. Where ablation deltas are
reported, the dense reference is recomputed on the same $10\%$ subsample
so that all deltas are internally consistent; single-run differences
below roughly one point (mAP@50 on VID, Top-1 on Kinetics-400) on these
subsamples should be read as within noise.

\paragraph{Detection model.}
The detector is ViTDet-B \cite{vitdet}, a plain ViT-B backbone \cite{vit}
with a standard detection head. The backbone has 12 blocks, with global
self-attention at blocks 3, 6, 9 and 12 and $14 \times 14$ windowed
attention elsewhere; tokens are 768-dimensional and attention uses 12 heads
with learned relative position embeddings. We evaluate at $672 \times 672$
resolution, which gives 1{,}764 tokens per frame. Every gated accelerator
in the detection evaluation (Eventful, STGT and layered PSM) runs at a
single common token budget of $k = 512$, roughly $29\%$ of these tokens.
We fix the budget once, for two reasons: $k = 512$ is the
operating point of the host's published sweep at which accuracy is nearly
saturated (within half an mAP point of dense at this resolution), so it is
the strongest host to layer on; and holding $k$ constant across methods
turns every comparison into a comparison of reuse mechanisms at equal
budget rather than of budget choices. The budget is stated here once and
applies to every detection table and figure below. Windowed blocks carry
relative-position biases, so PSM runs in its KV-reuse-only variant
there and in its full form on the global-attention blocks, which account
for most of the self-attention cost.

\paragraph{Protocol and cost accounting.}
All weights are frozen, pretrained checkpoints. PSM and every compared
method are applied as training-free, inference-time modifications, so the
comparison isolates the reuse mechanism itself.

\emph{Replay matching (detection).} Each VID video is processed twice. The first pass sees
only the first $W = 4$ frames; its sole purpose is to populate the
persistent memory, its predictions are discarded, and its FLOPs are not
counted. The second pass replays the entire video from frame 0 in matching
mode, and only this pass is scored and costed. This accounting is
conservative in the direction that hurts our method: mAP@50 covers every
frame, and the reported GFLOPs per frame is the steady-state cost of an
already-warm model, with no warm-up cost amortized in the method's favour.
The dense, Eventful, STGT and MaskVD baselines are evaluated on the same
frames under the same two-pass accounting, so every number in this section
is directly comparable. We state the scope of this protocol explicitly: it
models deployments in which content recurs after the memory is built, such
as archived or looped footage, re-analysis of stored streams, and
stationary-camera settings whose backgrounds persist across the warm-up
boundary. It does not model a strictly single-pass live stream over
never-repeated content, and we make no claims for that setting. The
action-recognition benchmark deliberately adopts the complementary
accounting, a single pass in which warm-up is paid in full and counted,
and we describe it in that subsection. Between them the two protocols
bracket the deployment space.

\emph{Layered instantiation.} Eventful Transformers \cite{eventful} serve
as the host throughout. An Eventful block gates the qkv input, the
attention-output projection and the MLP: it selects the top-$k$
most-changed tokens for recomputation and reuses buffered values for the
rest, with $k$ acting as its compute budget. The layered filter plugs in at
the qkv gate. It removes the fraction $\gamma$ of the gate's top-$k$
proposal that best matches the persistent memory and forces the surviving
set onto all three gates (Algorithm~\ref{alg:eventfultm}). At $\gamma = 0$
every gate receives exactly its Eventful proposal, so the pipeline reduces
to Eventful itself, and every layered comparison is controlled down to the
single knob $\gamma$.

\subsection{Comparison with the State of the Art}
\label{sec:eval-sota}

\paragraph{Accuracy--compute trade at a common budget.}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places all methods in the accuracy--compute
plane at the common token budget. The main result is a large and
uniform reduction in compute. Layered PSM runs at $46.6$ GFLOPs per
frame, the lowest of any method by a clear margin: $73\%$ below the dense
backbone, $23\%$ below Eventful, $32\%$ below STGT and $42\%$ below MaskVD.
The accuracy cost is small. PSM scores $79.87$ mAP@50, at most $2.4$
points below the most accurate method (dense, at $82.28$) and $1.9$ below
the strongest accelerator (Eventful, at $81.80$). Among the configurations
compared at this budget, no method is simultaneously cheaper and more
accurate, so PSM sits at the low-compute end of the observed
frontier, and it beats the spatially compounded Eventful variant on both
axes at once ($46.6$ vs.\ $52.8$ GFLOPs, $79.87$ vs.\ $79.48$ mAP@50).
Against STGT the compute margin is wide ($46.6$ vs.\ $68.5$ GFLOPs) at
comparable accuracy.

We scope this claim deliberately. It is a statement about the compared
configurations at $k = 512$, not about the full frontier each baseline
could trace by sweeping its own budget. In particular, Eventful can reach
the same compute point by lowering $k$: interpolating its published
budget--accuracy curve, an Eventful configuration matched to $46.6$ GFLOPs
would recover roughly $1.5$ mAP over PSM, and we plot this
matched-FLOPs point alongside ours in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} rather than
leaving it to prose. The two mechanisms are therefore complementary routes
into the low-compute regime, not a strict accuracy win for content-based
reuse. What the comparison establishes is narrower and still useful:
layered on the host at a fixed budget, persistent memory reaches a more
aggressive compute point than the host alone provides, while staying within
a couple of mAP points of the field. That trade is attractive wherever
compute, not the last point of accuracy, is the binding constraint.

\paragraph{Deployment cost: latency and memory.}
Table~\ref{tab:vid672} reports the same comparison with measured wall-clock
latency and peak memory, which tell a different story from FLOPs alone.

PSM consumes more memory than the other methods, as expected: it is the
only one that maintains an explicit content cache. The cost is bounded,
however. The memory is collapsed to a fixed-size set of prototypes at the
end of the caching window (Proposition~\ref{prop:cachesize}: $O(MC)$ per
block, independent of stream duration), so its size is governed by scene
complexity and does not grow with video length. The high absolute
footprint in the table (2329\,MB) is specific to the layered setting,
where the bounded cache stacks on the Eventful host's own reference and
accumulator buffers rather than replacing them; buffer sharing between
cache and host is the direct route to reducing it.

Latency is a milder story. At 60.0\,ms, PSM is slightly slower than
the gated baselines (STGT at 55.1\,ms, MaskVD at 54.9\,ms) but comparable
to them, and it is faster than the dense backbone (70.1\,ms). Two limits
deserve to be stated plainly. First, the most relevant comparison, against
the host Eventful's own $k = 512$ latency and memory, is not yet available
(Table~\ref{tab:vid672}), so we claim comparability with the gated
baselines we did measure, not latency parity with the host. Second, we
attribute the residual gap to unfused matching and gather/scatter kernels,
but we have not built a fused implementation, so this attribution is a
hypothesis rather than a demonstrated ceiling. The FLOP reduction is real;
whether it becomes a proportional wall-clock gain is engineering work that
lies outside this paper.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Reuse fraction $\gamma$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates, on the detection benchmark, the reuse
fraction $\gamma$, the single knob that determines how much of the host's
recompute set the persistent memory absorbs. All deltas in this section are computed against the dense
reference on the same $10\%$ subsample, so the comparisons are paired
throughout. Compute savings grow smoothly with $\gamma$, from $73.3\%$ to
$95.2\%$ relative to the dense backbone, as the effective token-skipping
ratio rises from $78.2\%$ to $98.5\%$. Accuracy, however, degrades
super-linearly rather than in proportion. The first step is nearly free: at
$\gamma = 0.25$ the filter discards a quarter of the proposed tokens for a
cost of $2.8$ mAP points, and this already delivers almost three quarters
of the achievable saving. Doubling the reuse fraction to $\gamma = 0.5$
costs $7.3$ points for only $7.8$ further points of saving. Beyond that the
exchange collapses: $\gamma = 0.75$ loses $18.6$ points and $\gamma = 0.95$
loses $44.0$, at which stage the detector has been degraded past usefulness
in exchange for a final $6$ points of compute. These losses are an order of
magnitude above the subsample's noise floor, so the shape of the curve is
not in question even from single runs. The practical operating range for
dense spatial prediction is therefore the low-$\gamma$ regime, and
$\gamma = 0.25$ is the configuration carried into the comparison above.

\paragraph{Prototype granularity ($T$ merge passes).}
The second knob is the number of merge passes $T$ used to compress the
accumulated tokens into cache prototypes. Unlike $\gamma$, $T$ changes only
which tokens the memory holds, not how many are recomputed, so it leaves
GFLOPs untouched at every operating point: it is a pure accuracy-side knob
and can be tuned for free. Its measured effect on accuracy is small and
non-monotonic. Sweeping $T \in \{2,4,6,8\}$ at fixed $\gamma$ moves mAP@50
by at most $1.2$ points in the usable range $\gamma \le 0.75$
($79.14$--$79.49$ at $\gamma = 0.25$; $74.35$--$75.02$ at $\gamma = 0.5$),
with no consistent direction. Because these spreads sit near the
single-run noise floor of the $64$-video subsample, we do not interpret
their ordering; the supported conclusion is an insensitivity result, in two
parts. First, the method is robust to this hyperparameter: there is no
fragile sweet spot, and the mid-range value $T = 6$ is a safe default,
being the per-$\gamma$ best or within $0.3$ points of it at every
low-$\gamma$ setting. Second, the near-indifference to prototype
granularity indicates that detection accuracy is governed by how many
tokens are replaced (set by $\gamma$), not by how finely the cache
summarises them. This is consistent with the frontier result: the accuracy
cost is intrinsic to substituting spatial tokens at all, not an artefact of
a coarse cache. The spread does widen once $\gamma$ reaches the degenerate
value $0.95$ ($\pm 2.8$ points), but that regime is already off the useful
part of the curve.

\paragraph{Task and model.}
The second benchmark moves to the opposite end of the readout spectrum:
video action recognition on Kinetics-400 \cite{kinetics400}, which
assigns one of 400 action classes to a short ($\sim$10\,s) video clip.
Where VID scores a prediction at every spatial location, here the entire
clip is summarised into a single classification read from a class token,
so this task probes how token substitution behaves when the output is
global rather than dense. We evaluate on the full 19{,}877-clip
validation split and report Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy.

The model is ViViT-B \cite{vivit} in its factorised form, in the
configuration of the Eventful host \cite{eventful}. Its spatial
sub-model is a ViT-B encoder of 12 blocks with 768-dimensional tokens and
12 heads; it prepends a class embedding token and uses neither windowed
attention nor relative position embeddings. That sub-model is applied
sequentially to the sixteen two-frame tubelet steps of a 32-frame view,
and a four-block temporal sub-model then classifies the resulting
sequence of per-step embeddings. Predictions are
averaged over 12 views per clip (4 temporal $\times$ 3 spatial crops) at
$224 \times 224$ resolution, which gives 197 tokens per step. The spatial
sub-model dominates the compute and is the component the host
accelerates. As on detection, $k$ is Eventful's token budget: the number
of most-changed tokens its gates select for recomputation on each step,
with buffered values reused for the rest. We evaluate the three budgets
of the host's published sweep, $k \in \{24, 48, 96\}$ of the 197 tokens
per step (the analogue of $k = 512$ of 1{,}764 on VID); a larger $k$
means more recomputation, hence higher accuracy at higher cost. Each
budget loads the host's published checkpoint, whose temporal sub-model
was fine-tuned on Eventful spatial outputs at that budget
\cite{eventful}. PSM is layered on top of these checkpoints with no
further training, exactly as on detection.

\paragraph{Single-pass protocol.}
This evaluation deliberately drops replay matching. Each clip is
processed exactly once, in temporal order: the first four temporal steps
of each view run through the host in caching mode and populate the
persistent memory, the remaining twelve run in matching mode, and every
operation of both phases is counted, so the reported cost per clip is the
sum of the caching and matching terms. Nothing is amortised away and no
frame is seen twice. This is the accounting a real deployment faces: a
model starts cold on a stream, pays for its own warm-up, and has to
recover that investment within the footage it actually sees. It is the
mirror image of the replay protocol used for detection, which isolates
the steady state of an already-warm model. Reporting both
regimes, each on the benchmark where it is most informative, brackets the
cost accounting rather than choosing the flattering end of it.

\paragraph{Aggressive reuse is free on a global readout.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400-ablation} repeats the reuse-fraction ablation on the
Kinetics-400 $10\%$ subsample (1{,}988 clips, $k = 24$). The contrast
with detection is stark. On VID, accuracy degraded super-linearly in
$\gamma$ and collapsed beyond $\gamma = 0.5$; here it is a step function.
Accuracy drops once, when the filter first engages, and is then flat to
within $0.1$ Top-1 points across the entire sweep
($58.75\% \rightarrow 58.70\%$ from $\gamma = 0.25$ to $\gamma = 0.95$)
while the matching cost falls by $12\times$ ($329 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs
per clip). Sweeping the merge-pass count $T$ at fixed $\gamma$ moved
Top-1 by less than the reporting precision on our grid, so, as on
detection, prototype granularity is a second-order effect; we carry
$T = 2$ forward. The mechanism is the readout: a class token aggregates
over all spatial tokens, so the perturbation introduced by substituting
mostly-static background tokens is largely averaged out before it reaches
the classification, whereas a dense detection head reads every
substituted location directly. The consequence is that the persistent
memory can be pushed far harder here than on detection: the selected
operating point is $\gamma = 0.95$, against $\gamma = 0.25$ on VID. That
makes global-readout video understanding the class of task persistent
reuse is most tailored to. The full-split runs confirm that
the step stays flat at scale: $\gamma = 0.5$ and $\gamma = 0.95$ score
$59.85\%$ and $59.82\%$ Top-1, respectively.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400} reports the full-split comparison across host
budgets at the selected operating point. At $k = 24$, layering PSM
on the host cuts the whole-clip cost by $39\%$
($1053 \rightarrow 645$ GFLOPs) for $2.6$ Top-1 points
($62.38\% \rightarrow 59.82\%$). It cuts the matching phase, which is the
entire marginal cost of processing further footage once the memory
exists, by $16\times$ ($435 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs). The favourable operating
point is the small budget: at $k = 48$ the one-time accuracy step is
larger ($67.54\% \rightarrow 58.37\%$), so the filtered model surrenders
the accuracy the extra budget had bought. \textbf{[TBD: $k = 96$ PSM
run.]} We also note an honest quirk of single-pass accounting: at
$k = 96$ the host's own whole-clip cost ($3525$ GFLOPs) exceeds the dense
backbone ($3360$), because on a 16-step clip the savings from gating do
not recover the cost of building and maintaining its reference buffers.
The matching column is the number that survives on longer footage.

\paragraph{The long-video reading.}
The split of Table~\ref{tab:k400} into caching and matching terms is the
point of the single-pass protocol. The caching term is paid once per
memory build, and in a 16-step clip it is amortised over only twelve
matched steps, the least favourable case this accounting can present.
On a long video or a streaming deployment, where the memory persists
across footage far longer than its caching window, the amortised cost
tends to the matching column alone: at $k = 24$, $\gamma = 0.95$, that is
$27$ GFLOPs per 16-step clip-equivalent, or about $2.3$ GFLOPs per
temporal step with all 12 views included. That is $94\%$ below the host's
own matching cost and roughly $1\%$ of the dense backbone. We state the scope of this
extrapolation plainly: the measured clips are ten seconds long, the
current implementation freezes the memory after the caching window, and
while the matching cost is constant by construction, accuracy under
long-horizon content drift is unmeasured. The matching column is
therefore a ceiling on streaming savings rather than a demonstrated
long-stream result; what makes it credible is that the per-clip accuracy
cost of reaching it is the $2.6$-point step already measured at full
scale.

\subsection{Summary and Scope of Findings}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

On VID at the common budget, layered PSM is the cheapest compared method
($46.6$ GFLOPs per frame, $73\%$ below dense and $23\%$ below Eventful)
for an accuracy cost of at most $2.4$ mAP@50; no compared configuration
is both cheaper and more accurate, though a budget-matched Eventful point
recovers roughly $1.5$ mAP, so the two mechanisms are complementary
rather than substitutes. Accuracy is governed almost entirely by the
reuse fraction $\gamma$, degrading super-linearly under the dense
detection readout, while the merge-pass count $T$ is FLOP-neutral and
nearly irrelevant. The cache is bounded by scene complexity, but stacking
it on the host's buffers makes it the highest-memory row, and the FLOP
saving is only partly realised in wall-clock terms.

Action recognition inverts this picture. Under a global class-token
readout accuracy is a step function of $\gamma$, so the memory can absorb
$\gamma = 0.95$ of the recompute proposal, cutting whole-clip cost by
$39\%$ and matching cost by $16\times$ for $2.6$ Top-1 points under fully
counted single-pass accounting. Persistent reuse is thus most tailored to
global-readout tasks, and the streaming implication is the extension we
consider most promising: on long streams the amortised cost tends to the
matching term alone, roughly $1\%$ of dense, and since matching already
computes token-to-prototype similarity, an additive cache update (folding
matched tokens into their prototypes, appending unexplained ones) would
keep the frozen memory current at negligible cost. If that sustains
accuracy over long horizons, it implies order-of-magnitude reductions on
streaming footage for a few points of accuracy; we leave this to future
work.

The scope of these claims: detection is compared at a single common
budget under a replay protocol that models recurring-content deployments;
action recognition uses single-pass accounting at three host budgets; and
operating points were selected on $10\%$ subsamples of the reported
splits, as is standard for these benchmarks.
