\begin{abstract}
Many transformer acceleration techniques rely on caching or short-term
computation reuse to avoid redundant computation. However, existing
caches are fundamentally identity-based: they reuse representations only
when the same token, spatial location, or recent temporal context is
encountered again. Consequently, recurring scene content is often
recomputed after camera motion or long temporal gaps. This raises a
fundamental question: can transformer inference exploit
\emph{content-addressable semantic caching}, retrieving previously
computed representations according to what they represent rather than
when or where they were computed?
%
To answer this question, we propose \emph{Persistent Semantic Memory}
(PSM), a training-free semantic caching algorithm that maintains a
compact content-addressable memory bank of recurring semantic
representations and their associated transformer activations. Unlike
conventional caches, PSM retrieves reusable representations based on
semantic similarity, enabling computation reuse across long temporal
intervals without retraining or architectural modifications.
%
Experiments on video classification and object detection show that
persistent semantic memory complements existing temporal reuse methods.
Layered on top of a state-of-the-art video transformer accelerator,
PSM reduces the remaining computation by up to 49\% while incurring
only a 2.5 percentage-point drop in Top-1 accuracy. More broadly, our results suggest
that persistent semantic memory is a new primitive for efficient
transformer inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
 
Transformer architectures have become the dominant paradigm for video understanding due to their ability to model long-range spatial and temporal dependencies through self-attention~\cite{vit,vivit,videomae}. Recent advances have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of video analysis tasks, including action recognition, video object detection, and scene understanding \cite{vivit,vitdet}. However, these improvements come at a significant computational cost: every incoming frame is transformed into hundreds of visual tokens, each of which undergoes multiple layers of expensive attention and feed-forward computation.
%
Despite this computational burden, real-world video streams exhibit a striking property that current inference pipelines largely ignore: \emph{semantic persistence}. Although cameras continuously capture new frames, the underlying scene often changes slowly. Buildings, roads, furniture, vegetation, and other background structures frequently remain visible for hundreds of frames, temporarily disappear due to camera motion or occlusion, and later reappear. Consequently, modern video transformers repeatedly reconstruct semantic representations of visual content they have already processed many times before.
 
 
A large body of recent work has sought to reduce the computational cost of video transformers by exploiting redundancy across nearby frames. Existing approaches dynamically prune tokens, reuse activations, or selectively recompute only regions that have changed since previous frames~\cite{eventful,stgt,deltacnn,maskvd,cstvit}. Although highly effective, these methods share an important underlying assumption: useful redundancy is fundamentally \emph{local in time}. Their decisions are based almost exclusively on comparisons with the immediately preceding frames, implicitly treating inference as a short-term temporal optimization problem.
%
However, semantic redundancy extends far beyond adjacent frames. Visual content frequently reappears after long temporal gaps or significant camera motion, long after conventional temporal buffers have discarded the corresponding representations. Existing acceleration methods possess short-term temporal memory, but lack a mechanism for persistent semantic memory.
%
This observation motivates a fundamental question:
 
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can video transformers remember previously understood scene content and reuse that knowledge whenever the same semantic information reappears, regardless of when or where it is observed?}
\end{quote}
 
 
 
To answer this question, we introduce \textbf{PSM}, a training-free inference framework that augments video transformers with a \emph{PSM cache}. Rather than determining whether a token has changed only with respect to recent frames, PSM asks a different question: \emph{has the model already understood this visual content before?}
%
PSM incrementally builds a compact memory of recurring semantic content observed during inference and reuses previously computed key-value representations whenever similar content reappears. Because memory retrieval is based on visual content rather than image coordinates, PSM naturally complements existing temporal redundancy methods, remaining effective even under camera motion or long-term scene recurrence. Furthermore, since PSM requires neither retraining nor architectural modifications, it can be layered on top of existing transformer acceleration techniques, compounding their computational savings.
 
 
 
Beyond proposing a new inference mechanism, this paper investigates a broader scientific question: \emph{when does persistent semantic memory improve efficient video understanding?} To answer this question, we evaluated PSM as a layer on top of existing temporal token-reduction methods across representative video classification and video object detection benchmarks. Our objective is to assess how PSM improves computational efficiency and to identify the conditions under which long-term semantic memory benefits transformer inference.
 
 
 
Our experiments show that persistent semantic memory improves the efficiency of video classification. When combined with an existing state-of-the-art temporal acceleration method, PSM reduces the remaining computation by up to 49\% while incurring only a 2.5 percentage-point drop in Top-1 accuracy, with detailed full-split results showing a 39\% whole-clip reduction at the favorable Kinetics-400 operating point and a 16$\times$ steady-state matching reduction. Interestingly, we also observe that the same mechanism is considerably less effective for dense prediction tasks such as object detection, where spatially localized representations are far more sensitive to approximation. PSM demonstrates that long-term semantic recurrence constitutes an additional and largely unexplored source of computational efficiency, complementing existing token pruning and temporal gating techniques rather than replacing them.
 
 
 
%These results suggest that efficient video inference should not be viewed solely as a problem of exploiting temporal redundancy. Instead, it is fundamentally a problem of \emph{remembering} previously acquired semantic knowledge. By introducing persistent scene memory into transformer inference, TempoMem demonstrates that long-term semantic recurrence constitutes an additional and largely unexplored source of computational efficiency, complementing existing token pruning and temporal gating techniques rather than replacing them.
 
%We believe persistent semantic memory opens a new direction for efficient video intelligence. Beyond transformer acceleration, similar ideas could enable continual visual understanding, lifelong perception, adaptive memory management, and memory-aware multimodal reasoning systems. We hope this work encourages future research that treats semantic memory as a first-class component of efficient AI inference rather than merely an implementation optimization.
 
 
 
\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}
 
 
\paragraph{Spatial token reduction.}
Spatial acceleration methods reduce computation within individual frames
by pruning, sampling, or merging uninformative tokens. DynamicViT
\cite{dynamicvit} and A-ViT \cite{avit} learn token-pruning policies;
EViT \cite{evit} and ATS \cite{ats} retain tokens according to estimated
importance; and ToMe \cite{tome} merges similar tokens without additional
training. These methods primarily address intra-frame redundancy and do
not retain reusable representations across a video stream. PSM uses
bipartite matching to summarize recurring content across frames into a
persistent memory, making it complementary to spatial token reduction.
 
\paragraph{Temporal computation reuse.}
Temporal acceleration methods reuse computation across nearby frames.
DeltaCNN \cite{deltacnn} propagates sparse frame differences, STGT
\cite{stgt} uses learned spatio-temporal gates, and Eventful Transformers
\cite{eventful} recompute only tokens that have changed substantially.
MaskVD \cite{maskvd} skips background regions, while CST-ViT
\cite{cstvit} combines spatial and temporal redundancy elimination.
These approaches primarily rely on recent, often position-aligned
observations. PSM instead retrieves representations by content from
its PSM cache, enabling reuse after spatial displacement or
longer temporal gaps. In its layered mode, PSM filters the
recomputation set proposed by an existing accelerator; we instantiate
this composition with Eventful Transformers.
 
\paragraph{Memory-augmented video transformers.}
MeMViT \cite{memvit} stores compressed representations of past clips to
extend temporal context, while Token Turing Machines \cite{ttm} learn an
external memory for sequential visual reasoning. These methods use memory
to improve prediction by providing additional historical context.
PSM instead uses memory to reduce inference cost: it stores compact
prototypes of previously processed content and reuses their transformer
representations when similar content reappears. It requires no additional
training and leaves the underlying model unchanged.
 
\paragraph{Positioning of PSM.}
Existing work therefore addresses intra-frame redundancy, short-term
temporal reuse, or memory for predictive context. PSM targets a
different setting: \emph{persistent, content-based reuse for efficient
inference}. It maintains a bounded PSM cache and avoids recomputing
visual content that was processed earlier in the stream, even when it
reappears at a different location or after a temporal gap.
 

 
\section{Persistent Semantic Memory for Efficient Inference} 
\label{sec:method}
 
Many transformer acceleration methods rely on caching or computation reuse. However, existing caches are fundamentally identity-based, i.e., cached representations are reused only when the same token, spatial location, or recent temporal context is encountered again. This naturally raises a broader question:
\emph{can transformer inference exploit content-addressable semantic
caching instead?} In particular, can previously computed internal
representations be indexed by the visual content they encode, rather than
by when or where they were computed?
 
We hypothesize that a substantial fraction of transformer computation is
associated with recurring scene content rather than individual frames.
Consequently, if semantically equivalent visual content induces
sufficiently similar internal representations, those representations can
be retrieved and reused in place of redundant computation, even after
camera motion or long temporal gaps.
%
PSM is built upon this hypothesis. Rather than caching
representations according to token identity or temporal locality, it
constructs a compact \emph{PSM cache} that is queried
by content. During inference, each transformer block summarizes recurring
visual representations into semantic prototypes and associates them with
their corresponding transformer activations. Incoming tokens retrieve
these cached representations through content similarity, allowing
previous computations to be reused whenever the memory sufficiently
explains the observed scene. Because the memory is independent of the
underlying model parameters, PSM requires no retraining and operates
as a lightweight layer on top of existing temporal acceleration
methods.
 
 
\subsection{PSM Cache}
 
This semantic caching hypothesis requires a memory that can retrieve previously computed transformer representations based on content rather than token identity or temporal locality. PSM realizes this idea through a \emph{PSM cache}, a compact content-addressable memory that stores reusable semantic representations accumulated throughout
inference.
 
Unlike conventional caches, whose entries are indexed by position or
execution order, the PSM cache organizes memory according to semantic similarity.
Consequently, visually similar scene content can retrieve previously
computed representations even when it reappears at different locations or
after long temporal gaps. To remain practical for long video sequences,
the memory must remain compact, support efficient retrieval, and evolve
online without requiring retraining.
%
We satisfy these requirements by representing the PSM cache as a set of semantic
prototypes, each associated with the transformer representations it
encodes. The following subsection describes how these prototypes are
constructed and maintained during inference.
 
\subsection{Memory Construction} 
The PSM cache is constructed online during inference. Since no semantic memory is
available at the beginning of a video, PSM first observes a short
bootstrap segment to initialize the memory. Rather than storing every
token representation, the PSM cache maintains a compact set of semantic prototypes
that summarize recurring scene content.
%
For each token representation $\mathbf{z}_i$ produced by the current
transformer block, PSM measures its similarity to the existing
prototypes: $s_j = \mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_j)$,
where $\mathbf{p}_j$ denotes the $j$-th prototype and
$\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is cosine similarity.
%
If the maximum similarity exceeds a threshold $\tau$, the corresponding
prototype is updated to incorporate the new observation; otherwise, a new
prototype is created. Consequently, the memory grows according to the
semantic diversity of the video rather than its duration.
 
Each prototype stores not only its semantic representation, but also the
transformer activations associated with it, enabling their subsequent
reuse during inference. The next subsection describes how incoming token
representations query the PSM cache to retrieve previously computed activations.
 
 
 
\subsection{Memory Retrieval}
 
Once the PSM cache has been initialized, every incoming token first queries the
memory before executing the standard transformer computations. Given its
semantic representation $\mathbf{z}_i$, PSM retrieves the most
similar prototype,
%
\begin{equation}
j^*=\arg\max_j \; \mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_j),
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{p}_j$ denotes the $j$-th semantic prototype.
 
The similarity score determines whether the cached representation
provides a reliable explanation of the current scene content. If
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_{j^*}) \ge \tau,
\end{equation}
%
the retrieved prototype is considered semantically equivalent to the
incoming token and its associated transformer activations become
available for reuse. Otherwise, the token follows the standard inference
path, and its representation may subsequently be incorporated into the PSM cache as
described in the previous subsection.
 
This retrieval mechanism extends conventional caching from exact
identity-based reuse to approximate semantic reuse, allowing previously
computed representations to remain useful even when recurring scene
content appears at different spatial locations or after long temporal
gaps. The next subsection describes how the retrieved activations are
integrated into the transformer computation.
 
 
 
\subsection{Representation Reuse}
 
Memory retrieval determines whether an incoming token is semantically
equivalent to previously observed scene content. When this condition is
satisfied, PSM reuses the transformer representations associated
with the retrieved prototype instead of recomputing them.
%
Concretely, the matched prototype replaces the corresponding semantic
representation, while its cached key and value projections are injected
directly into the attention computation. Only unmatched tokens require
fresh projection and attention computation, allowing the transformer to
preserve prediction quality while substantially reducing redundant
operations.

 
\subsection{Layered Integration}
 
The semantic caching principle is independent of the underlying
inference strategy. Rather than replacing existing efficient
transformers,
PSM complements them by providing an additional source of semantic
reuse. Given any host acceleration method that selects a subset of tokens for recomputation, PSM first determines whether those tokens can be explained by its PSM cache. Tokens that are successfully matched are substituted or skipped according to the host's execution model, while the remaining tokens follow the original inference path. Consequently, PSM augments existing acceleration techniques without modifying their optimization objectives or training procedures. Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered} summarizes this layered execution strategy.
 
 
 
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Layered Semantic Memory Inference}
\label{alg:layered}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require
  Frame tokens; host method with its per-frame token selection;
  prototype cache $E$; buffer $B$; reuse fraction $\gamma$
 
\State $C \gets$ recompute set of $k$ tokens proposed by the host
  \hfill\Comment{Tokens the host flags as useful}
\If{phase is \textsc{caching}}
  \State Append the tokens of $C$ to $B$; on the last caching frame,
    merge $B$ with $T$ bipartite soft-matching passes into $E$
  \State \Return standard host output on $C$
\Else
  \State Compare each candidate to every prototype in $E$
    by cosine similarity
  \State $D \gets$ the $\lfloor \gamma k \rfloor$ candidates most similar
    to a prototype
    \hfill\Comment{Special tokens always protected}
  \State $R \gets C \setminus D$
    \hfill\Comment{Kept set: tokens the cache cannot explain}
  \If{substitution variant}
    \State Host recomputes all of $C$, with each input in $D$
      overwritten by its best-matching prototype
  \Else
    \State Host recomputes only $R$
  \EndIf
  \State Dropped tokens follow the host's existing reuse path
  \State \Return host output
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
 
  
 
\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}
 
PSM introduces an additional semantic retrieval step before each transformer block. Its computational benefit therefore depends on a simple trade-off: the cost of retrieving reusable representations from the PSM cache versus the cost of recomputing those representations using the original transformer block.
%
We analyze the computational complexity of a single transformer block during inference and compare the proposed approach against \emph{dense inference}, i.e., the standard execution in which every input token is processed normally without any representation reuse. For an input containing $N$ tokens with embedding dimension $C$, the dominant floating-point operations (FLOPs) of dense inference are
approximately:
 
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{dense}}
\approx
4NC^2
+
8NC^2
+
4N^2C,
\end{equation}
 
where the first term corresponds to the query, key, value, and output linear projections, the second term accounts for the two-layer feed-forward network, and the final quadratic term captures the self-attention computation.
 During a matching frame, PSM replaces a fraction of the background tokens with cached semantic representations, so only the
remaining unmatched tokens require fresh key/value computation. The
resulting cost becomes:
 
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{PSM}}
\approx
10N'C^2
+
2(N'-M_u)C^2
+
4N'^2C
+
N_cMC,
\end{equation}
 
where $N'$ is the number of tokens processed after substitution,
$M_u$ is the number of tokens whose key/value projections are reused,
$N_c$ is the number of candidate tokens compared against memory, and
$M$ is the number of semantic prototypes. The final term is the semantic
retrieval cost. Since $N'<N$, the
dominant attention computation is reduced quadratically, while all
pointwise operations scale with the reduced token count.
 
Moreover, semantic retrieval quickly amortizes its own overhead.
Matching requires $O(N_cM)$ similarity computations, whereas every
matched token avoids recomputing its key/value projections. Retrieval is therefore beneficial whenever $M < 2\gamma C$,
where $\gamma$ is the reuse fraction. For a typical ViT embedding dimension
($C=768$) and $\gamma=0.75$, the break-even point is $M<1152$ prototypes.
PSM typically operates with one to two orders of magnitude fewer
prototypes, so the retrieval cost is negligible compared to the
computation it eliminates.
 
Finally, unlike temporal window caches, whose memory grows linearly with
the number of stored frames, the PSM cache has a fixed
capacity. Each transformer block stores $O(MC)$ values, yielding
$O(LMC)$ memory for an $L$-layer transformer, independent of the length
of the video stream.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Settings and Details}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and metrics.}
Our primary benchmark is video object detection on ImageNet VID
\cite{imagenetvid}: dense
spatial prediction is the hardest case for token substitution. We
report mAP@50 on the full 639-video validation split, following
\cite{eventful,maskvd}. Two companion evaluations broaden the scope:
action recognition with ViViT on Kinetics-400, read
globally from a class token, as discussed in the Video Action Recognition
subsection, and
the same detection benchmark at $1024 \times 1024$, which tests a fixed
recompute budget against a much larger frame. GFLOPs are measured with in-code operation counters, so every
reported reduction includes the cost of PSM's own matching; latency is CUDA-event
wall-clock time and memory is peak GPU allocation, measured on one
NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 under an identical warm-up protocol.

\paragraph{Ablation split.}
Ablations use a fixed $10\%$ subsample of each dataset (64 VID videos,
1{,}988 Kinetics-400 clips), drawn once and shared by every
configuration; all comparisons are paired against a dense reference on
the same subsample. The subsamples come from the validation
splits, since neither benchmark has a tuning partition. The operating points carried into the full-split
comparisons ($\gamma = 0.25$, $T = 6$ for detection; $\gamma = 0.95$,
$T = 2$ for recognition) were fixed on the subsamples beforehand;
single-run differences below roughly one point are within noise.

\paragraph{Detection model.}
The detector is ViTDet-B \cite{vitdet}: a ViT-B backbone \cite{vit}
with global self-attention at blocks 3, 6, 9 and 12 and
$14 \times 14$ windowed attention elsewhere. The primary resolution is
$672 \times 672$ (1{,}764 tokens per frame); the companion evaluation
uses $1024 \times 1024$ (4{,}096). Every gated accelerator (Eventful,
STGT, layered PSM) runs at a single common token budget of $k = 512$
at both resolutions, i.e., $29\%$ of the tokens at $672$ and $12.5\%$
at $1024$. The budget is fixed where the host's published
sweep is nearly saturated, comparing reuse mechanisms at equal budget;
the same absolute $k$ deliberately tightens the relative budget at
$1024$. Windowed blocks carry
relative-position biases, so PSM runs there in KV-reuse-only form and
in full on the global-attention blocks.

\paragraph{Protocol and cost accounting.}
All weights are frozen pretrained checkpoints; PSM and every baseline
are training-free, inference-time modifications.
%
\emph{Replay matching (detection).} Each VID video is processed twice:
the first pass sees only the first $W = 4$ frames and populates the
memory (unscored, uncounted); the second replays the full video in
matching mode and is the only pass scored and costed. mAP@50 thus covers
every frame and the reported cost is the warm steady state; all
baselines use the same accounting. The
protocol models deployments in which content recurs after the memory
is built (archived or looped footage, stationary cameras), not a
single-pass live stream; the recognition benchmark adopts the
complementary accounting, bracketing the deployment space.
%
\emph{Layered instantiation.} Eventful Transformers \cite{eventful}
serve as the host throughout. The layered filter plugs in at the QKV
gate: it removes the fraction $\gamma$ of the gate's top-$k$ proposal
that best matches the memory and forces the surviving set onto all
three gates (Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered}). At $\gamma = 0$ the pipeline
reduces to Eventful itself.

\subsection{Comparison with the State of the Art}
\label{sec:eval-sota}

This comparison tests the paper's central hypothesis: that at an equal
recompute budget, layering persistent content-addressable reuse on a
state-of-the-art temporal accelerator removes a further share of the
residual computation at only a small accuracy cost.

\paragraph{Accuracy--compute trade at a common budget.}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places all methods in the accuracy--compute
plane at $672 \times 672$. Layered PSM runs at $46.6$ GFLOPs per
frame, the lowest of any method: $73\%$ below the dense backbone,
$23\%$ below Eventful, $32\%$ below STGT and $42\%$ below MaskVD. The
accuracy cost is small, $79.87$ mAP@50, at most $2.4$ points below any
compared configuration, and no configuration is simultaneously cheaper
and more accurate; PSM also beats the spatially compounded Eventful
variant on both axes at once. This holds for the compared configurations at $k = 512$, not the full
frontier each baseline could trace: a budget-matched Eventful
configuration interpolated from its published sweep would recover
roughly $1.5$ mAP (open marker in
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). The two mechanisms are therefore
complementary routes into the low-compute regime.

\paragraph{Deployment cost: latency and memory.}
The upper block of Table~\ref{tab:vid} adds measured latency and peak
memory. PSM has the highest footprint, as the only method with an
explicit content cache, but the host's own buffers already
account for 2324\,MB of it: the prototype cache adds only
about 5\,MB and does not grow with video length: the footprint is a
property of the layered setting, not the persistent memory. Latency is near parity with the host (60.0 vs.\ 58.9\,ms),
slightly behind the other gated baselines and well ahead of dense
(70.1\,ms); we attribute the gap between the $23\%$ FLOP reduction and
wall-clock parity to unfused matching and gather/scatter kernels, unverified
absent a fused implementation.

\paragraph{Scaling to $1024 \times 1024$.}
In the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:vid} the compute advantage widens
while the accuracy picture tightens. Eventful itself is now measured
at $1024$: $87.8$ GFLOPs per frame, $145.2$\,ms and $8045$\,MB at
$79.42$ mAP@50. PSM runs at $69.1$ GFLOPs per
frame, $21\%$ below this reference, $85\%$ below dense (vs.\ $73\%$ at $672$) and $58\%$ below
STGT, the expected effect of gating at a fixed absolute budget as the
frame grows. But PSM loses the most accuracy of the methods measured at both
resolutions, $4.3$ points ($79.87 \rightarrow 75.57$), landing $1.4$
points below Eventful with spatial pooling at the same compute ($69.1$
vs.\ $70.9$ GFLOPs) and $3.85$ points below plain Eventful at $21\%$
lower compute: the double advantage observed at $672$ does not hold under the tighter $1024$ budget. We interpret this as the dense-readout limit reasserting itself: at
a $12.5\%$ budget the filter substitutes tokens the detection head
reads directly, so re-tuning $\gamma$ per resolution is the obvious
next step. Memory grows to $8062$\,MB, about $17$\,MB above the
Eventful reference, consistent with the small persistent-cache
overhead seen at $672$; latency follows the $672$ pattern, near
parity with the host ($143.0$ vs.\ $145.2$\,ms), $19\%$
faster than dense and slower than the other gated methods.

\paragraph{Main implication.}
The hypothesis is confirmed at $672$: layered PSM is the cheapest
compared configuration for at most $2.4$ mAP@50 and a few megabytes of
length-independent cache. It is conditional at $1024$: the compute
margin widens but accuracy dominance is lost, so the operating point
must track the relative budget.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Reuse fraction $\gamma$.}
\emph{Hypothesis.} The reuse fraction $\gamma$ sets how much of the
host's recompute set the memory absorbs, trading exact recomputation
for approximate, prototype-based substitution. This experiment asks how
a dense prediction head, which reads every spatial token directly,
tolerates that approximation; we hypothesize a low-$\gamma$ regime with
large savings at small accuracy cost, degrading steeply beyond it.
%
\emph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} sweeps $\gamma$ on the
detection subsample. Compute savings grow
smoothly with $\gamma$, from $73.3\%$ to $95.2\%$ of dense, but
accuracy degrades super-linearly: $\gamma = 0.25$ costs $2.8$ mAP
points and already delivers almost three quarters of the achievable
saving; $\gamma = 0.5$ costs $7.3$ points for only $7.8$ further
points of saving; $\gamma = 0.75$ and $0.95$ lose $18.6$ and $44.0$.
These losses are an order of magnitude above the noise floor.
%
\emph{Main implication.} Confirmed: under a dense readout, degradation
is super-linear in $\gamma$, so only the low-$\gamma$ regime is usable;
$\gamma = 0.25$ captures most of the saving for $2.8$ mAP points and is
carried into the comparison above.

\paragraph{Prototype granularity ($T$ merge passes).}
\emph{Hypothesis.} \emph{Prototype granularity} is how finely the cache
summarizes the tokens it stores: prototypes are built by $T$ passes of
bipartite soft matching (Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered}), each merging the
most similar pairs, so small $T$ yields many fine prototypes close to
the raw tokens and large $T$ few coarse averages. Since $T$ changes
only \emph{which} representations the memory holds, not \emph{how many}
tokens are recomputed, it is FLOP-neutral and isolates substitution
fidelity from reuse volume. We hypothesize accuracy is sensitive to
this fidelity, i.e., an optimal intermediate $T$ exists between
over-blurred and needlessly fine summaries.
%
\emph{Results.} Sweeping $T \in \{2,4,6,8\}$ at fixed $\gamma$ moves
mAP@50 by at most $1.2$ points in the usable range $\gamma \le 0.75$,
with no consistent direction and near the noise floor.
%
\emph{Main implication.} Rejected: there is no fragile sweet spot.
Accuracy is governed by how many tokens are replaced ($\gamma$), not
how finely the cache summarizes them ($T$); $T = 6$ is a safe default
requiring no tuning.

\subsection{Video Action Recognition}
\label{sec:eval-vivit}

\paragraph{Task, model and protocol.}
The second benchmark, action recognition on Kinetics-400 \cite{k400},
probes the opposite end of the readout spectrum: the whole clip is
summarized into one classification read from a class token. It tests
the hypothesis that tolerance to substitution is governed by the
\emph{readout structure}: a global readout averaging all spatial tokens
should tolerate far more aggressive reuse than a dense head. We
report Top-1/Top-5 on the full 19{,}877-clip validation split. The
model is factorized ViViT-B \cite{vivit} in the Eventful
configuration: a ViT-B spatial encoder applied to the sixteen
two-frame tubelet steps of a 32-frame view, a four-block temporal
classifier, and 12 views per clip at $224 \times 224$ (197 tokens per
step). We evaluate the host's three published budgets
$k \in \{24, 48, 96\}$, loading its published checkpoints (temporal
sub-model fine-tuned per budget); PSM is layered on top with no
further training. This evaluation deliberately drops replay matching:
each clip is processed once, the first four temporal steps in caching
mode and the remaining twelve in matching mode, with every operation of
both phases counted: the model starts cold and pays its own warm-up,
the mirror image of the detection protocol.

\paragraph{Aggressive reuse is well tolerated under a global readout.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400-ablation} repeats the $\gamma$ ablation
($k = 24$ subsample). Where detection degraded super-linearly, here
accuracy is a step function: it drops once when the filter engages and
is then flat to within $0.1$ Top-1 percentage points across the sweep
($58.75\% \rightarrow 58.70\%$ from $\gamma = 0.25$ to $0.95$) while
the matching cost falls by $12\times$ ($329 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs per
clip); prototype granularity is again second-order, and $T = 2$ is
carried forward. The mechanism is the readout: a class token averages over all spatial
tokens, so background-substitution perturbations largely cancel before
the classification, unlike a dense head that reads every substituted
location. The memory can therefore be pushed to $\gamma = 0.95$,
against $0.25$ on VID; the full split confirms the flat step
($59.85\%$ and $59.82\%$ Top-1 at $\gamma = 0.5$ and $0.95$).

\paragraph{Results and the long-video reading.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400} reports the full-split comparison. At $k = 24$,
layering PSM cuts the whole-clip cost by $39\%$
($1053 \rightarrow 645$ GFLOPs) for $2.6$ Top-1 percentage points
($62.38\% \rightarrow 59.82\%$), and cuts the matching phase, the
marginal cost of further footage once the memory exists, by $16\times$
($435 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs). The favorable operating point is the
small budget: at $k = 48$ the accuracy step is larger
($67.54\% \rightarrow 58.37\%$), surrendering what the extra budget
had bought. At $k = 96$ the pattern continues: PSM drops Top-1 to
$58.43\%$ ($75.71\% \rightarrow 58.43\%$, a $17.3$ percentage-point cost, the
largest of the three budgets) while cutting whole-clip cost by
$46\%$ ($3525 \rightarrow 1912$ GFLOPs) and the matching term by
$17\times$ ($1711 \rightarrow 98$ GFLOPs); across all three budgets
PSM's Top-1 settles in a narrow $58$--$60\%$ band regardless of the
host's own accuracy, reinforcing that the small budget is the
favorable operating point for layering. A caveat of single-pass accounting: at $k = 96$ the
host's own whole-clip cost ($3525$ GFLOPs) exceeds dense ($3360$),
since a 16-step clip cannot recover the cost of its reference buffers;
the matching column is the number that survives on longer footage.
%
The caching term is paid once and, in a 16-step clip, amortized over
only twelve matched steps, the least favorable case. On long footage the
amortized cost tends to the matching column alone: $27$ GFLOPs per
clip-equivalent at $k = 24$, $\gamma = 0.95$, about $2.3$ GFLOPs per
temporal step with all views, $94\%$ below the host's matching cost
and roughly $1\%$ of dense. Scope: clips are ten seconds, the
memory is frozen after the caching window, and accuracy under
long-horizon drift is unmeasured, so the matching column is a ceiling,
not a demonstrated long-stream result; its credibility rests on the
$2.6$-point step measured at full scale.

\paragraph{Main implication.}
The readout hypothesis is confirmed: under a global readout the
accuracy cost is a one-time step, flat in $\gamma$, so $\gamma = 0.95$
at the small host budget buys a $39\%$ whole-clip and $16\times$
marginal-cost reduction for $2.6$ Top-1 percentage points, while larger host
budgets are less effective under the layered setting.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced Persistent Semantic Memory (PSM), extending
transformer caching from identity-based to \emph{content-addressable}
reuse. Our contributions are: (i) a training-free mechanism that
summarizes recurring content into a compact prototype cache built
online and injects cached key/value projections directly into
attention; (ii) a layered interface composing PSM with any accelerator
exposing a recomputation set, leaving the host untouched; and (iii) a
complexity analysis showing retrieval amortizes its overhead whenever
$M < 2\gamma C$, a bound our caches undershoot by orders of magnitude, with
memory independent of video length.

The experiments delineate both the strength and the boundary of the
idea. On ImageNet VID at a matched budget, layered PSM is the cheapest
compared configuration ($46.6$ GFLOPs per frame, $73\%$ below dense,
$23\%$ below its Eventful host) within $2.4$ mAP@50 of every baseline,
composing with rather than substituting for the host's budget tuning.
Ablations show accuracy is governed almost entirely by the reuse
fraction $\gamma$, with prototype granularity $T$ FLOP-neutral and
second-order, and that the $\gamma$--accuracy curve is set by the task
readout: super-linear degradation under a dense detection head, but a
flat one-time step under ViViT's global class-token readout, where
$\gamma = 0.95$ cuts whole-clip cost by $39\%$ and marginal matching
cost by $16\times$ for $2.6$ Top-1 percentage points at full scale.

These results establish long-term semantic recurrence as an
additional, previously unexploited source of inference efficiency,
with readout structure as the key predictor of applicability. The most
promising extension is streaming, where the amortized cost tends to
the matching term alone, roughly $1\%$ of dense, and an additive cache
update would keep the frozen memory current; findings hold within the
stated scope of budgets, protocols and subsample-selected operating
points.
