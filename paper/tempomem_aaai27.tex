\begin{abstract}
Many transformer acceleration techniques rely on caching or short-term
computation reuse to avoid redundant computation. However, existing
caches are fundamentally identity-based: they reuse representations only
when the same token, spatial location, or recent temporal context is
encountered again. Consequently, recurring scene content is often
recomputed after camera motion or long temporal gaps. This raises a
fundamental question: can transformer inference exploit
\emph{content-addressable semantic caching}, retrieving previously
computed representations according to what they represent rather than
when or where they were computed?
%
To answer this question, we propose \emph{Persistent Semantic Memory}
(PSM), a training-free semantic caching algorithm that maintains a
compact content-addressable memory bank of recurring semantic
representations and their associated transformer activations. Unlike
conventional caches, PSM retrieves reusable representations based on
semantic similarity, enabling computation reuse across long temporal
intervals without retraining or architectural modifications.
%
Experiments on video classification and object detection show that
persistent semantic memory complements existing temporal reuse methods.
Layered on top of a state-of-the-art video transformer accelerator,
PSM reduces the remaining computation by up to 49\% while incurring
only a 2.5\% loss in Top-1 accuracy. More broadly, our results suggest
that persistent semantic memory is a new primitive for efficient
transformer inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

%\paragraph{Context.}
Transformer architectures have become the dominant paradigm for video understanding due to their ability to model long-range spatial and temporal dependencies through self-attention~\cite{vit,vivit,videomae}. Recent advances have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of video analysis tasks, including action recognition, video object detection, and scene understanding \cite{vivit,vitdet}. However, these improvements come at a significant computational cost: every incoming frame is transformed into hundreds of visual tokens, each of which undergoes multiple layers of expensive attention and feed-forward computation.
%
Despite this computational burden, real-world video streams exhibit a striking property that current inference pipelines largely ignore: \emph{semantic persistence}. Although cameras continuously capture new frames, the underlying scene often changes slowly. Buildings, roads, furniture, vegetation, and other background structures frequently remain visible for hundreds of frames, temporarily disappear due to camera motion or occlusion, and later reappear. Consequently, modern video transformers repeatedly reconstruct semantic representations of visual content they have already processed many times before.


%\paragraph{Need.}
A large body of recent work has sought to reduce the computational cost of video transformers by exploiting redundancy across nearby frames. Existing approaches dynamically prune tokens, reuse activations, or selectively recompute only regions that have changed since previous frames~\cite{eventful,stgt,deltacnn,maskvd,cstvit}. Although highly effective, these methods share an important underlying assumption: useful redundancy is fundamentally \emph{local in time}. Their decisions are based almost exclusively on comparisons with the immediately preceding frames, implicitly treating inference as a short-term temporal optimization problem.
%
However, semantic redundancy extends far beyond adjacent frames. Visual content frequently reappears after long temporal gaps or significant camera motion, long after conventional temporal buffers have discarded the corresponding representations. Existing acceleration methods possess short-term temporal memory, but lack a mechanism for persistent semantic memory.
%
This observation motivates a fundamental question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can video transformers remember previously understood scene content and reuse that knowledge whenever the same semantic information reappears, regardless of when or where it is observed?}
\end{quote}


%\paragraph{Task.}

To answer this question, we introduce \textbf{TempoMem}, a training-free inference framework that augments video transformers with a \emph{Persistent Scene Memory (PSM)}. Rather than determining whether a token has changed only with respect to recent frames, TempoMem asks a different question: \emph{has the model already understood this visual content before?}
%
TempoMem incrementally builds a compact memory of recurring semantic content observed during inference and reuses previously computed key-value representations whenever similar content reappears. Because memory retrieval is based on visual content rather than image coordinates, TempoMem naturally complements existing temporal redundancy methods, remaining effective even under camera motion or long-term scene recurrence. Furthermore, since TempoMem requires neither retraining nor architectural modifications, it can be layered on top of existing transformer acceleration techniques, compounding their computational savings.


%\paragraph{Object.}

Beyond proposing a new inference mechanism, this paper investigates a broader scientific question: \emph{when does persistent semantic memory improve efficient video understanding?} To answer this question, we evaluated TempoMem both as a standalone accelerator and as a layer on top of existing temporal token-reduction methods across representative video classification and video object detection benchmarks. Our evaluation objective has been to assess how TempoMem improves computational efficiency and understand the conditions under which long-term semantic memory benefits transformer inference.


%\paragraph{Findings.}

Our experiments show that persistent semantic memory improves the efficiency of video classification. When combined with an existing state-of-the-art temporal acceleration method, TempoMem reduces the remaining computation by up to 75\% while incurring only a small loss in classification accuracy. Interestingly, we also observe that the same mechanism is considerably less effective for dense prediction tasks such as object detection, where spatially localized representations are far more sensitive to approximation. TempoMem demonstrates that long-term semantic recurrence constitutes an additional and largely unexplored source of computational efficiency, complementing existing token pruning and temporal gating techniques rather than replacing them.


%\paragraph{Meaning.}

%These results suggest that efficient video inference should not be viewed solely as a problem of exploiting temporal redundancy. Instead, it is fundamentally a problem of \emph{remembering} previously acquired semantic knowledge. By introducing persistent scene memory into transformer inference, TempoMem demonstrates that long-term semantic recurrence constitutes an additional and largely unexplored source of computational efficiency, complementing existing token pruning and temporal gating techniques rather than replacing them.

%\paragraph{Perspective.}
%We believe persistent semantic memory opens a new direction for efficient video intelligence. Beyond transformer acceleration, similar ideas could enable continual visual understanding, lifelong perception, adaptive memory management, and memory-aware multimodal reasoning systems. We hope this work encourages future research that treats semantic memory as a first-class component of efficient AI inference rather than merely an implementation optimization.



\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}


\paragraph{Spatial token reduction.}
Spatial acceleration methods reduce computation within individual frames
by pruning, sampling, or merging uninformative tokens. DynamicViT
\cite{dynamicvit} and A-ViT \cite{avit} learn token-pruning policies;
EViT \cite{evit} and ATS \cite{ats} retain tokens according to estimated
importance; and ToMe \cite{tome} merges similar tokens without additional
training. These methods primarily address intra-frame redundancy and do
not retain reusable representations across a video stream. TempoMem uses
bipartite matching to summarize recurring content across frames into a
persistent memory, making it complementary to spatial token reduction.

\paragraph{Temporal computation reuse.}
Temporal acceleration methods reuse computation across nearby frames.
DeltaCNN \cite{deltacnn} propagates sparse frame differences, STGT
\cite{stgt} uses learned spatio-temporal gates, and Eventful Transformers
\cite{eventful} recompute only tokens that have changed substantially.
MaskVD \cite{maskvd} skips background regions, while CST-ViT
\cite{cstvit} combines spatial and temporal redundancy elimination.
These approaches primarily rely on recent, often position-aligned
observations. TempoMem instead retrieves representations by content from
a persistent scene memory, enabling reuse after spatial displacement or
longer temporal gaps. In its layered mode, TempoMem filters the
recomputation set proposed by an existing accelerator; we instantiate
this composition with Eventful Transformers.

\paragraph{Memory-augmented video transformers.}
MeMViT \cite{memvit} stores compressed representations of past clips to
extend temporal context, while Token Turing Machines \cite{ttm} learn an
external memory for sequential visual reasoning. These methods use memory
to improve prediction by providing additional historical context.
TempoMem instead uses memory to reduce inference cost: it stores compact
prototypes of previously processed content and reuses their transformer
representations when similar content reappears. It requires no additional
training and leaves the underlying model unchanged.

\paragraph{Positioning of TempoMem.}
Existing work therefore addresses intra-frame redundancy, short-term
temporal reuse, or memory for predictive context. TempoMem targets a
different setting: \emph{persistent, content-based reuse for efficient
inference}. It maintains a bounded scene memory and avoids recomputing
visual content that was processed earlier in the stream, even when it
reappears at a different location or after a temporal gap.

remains aligned, but it cannot directly recover semantic computations

reappears after camera motion, occlusion, or a long temporal gap may be
computed again even when a semantically equivalent representation was
observed earlier.

\emph{content-addressable, persistent reuse}. Instead of asking only
whether a token has changed at its current position, TempoMem asks
whether semantically similar content already exists anywhere in the

first proposes the tokens that should be recomputed, and TempoMem then
removes candidates already represented by persistent memory. Thus, the
host identifies recent change, while TempoMem recognizes long-term
semantic recurrence. We instantiate this composition with Eventful
Transformers \cite{eventful}, but the interface is applicable to methods
that expose an explicit recomputation set.

\paragraph{Memory-augmented video understanding.}
Memory has also been incorporated into video transformers to extend
their temporal receptive field and improve prediction quality. MeMViT
\cite{memvit} stores compressed representations of past clips to support
long-term video recognition, while Token Turing Machines \cite{ttm}
learn an external token memory that can be read from and written to
during sequential visual reasoning. In these methods, memory serves as
additional context: past information is retained because it may improve
the model's future predictions.

TempoMem uses memory for a different objective. Its Persistent Scene
Memory is not introduced to provide additional evidence to the model,
but to identify semantic computations that need not be performed again.
It stores compact prototypes of previously processed scene content
together with their reusable transformer representations, leaves the
underlying model parameters unchanged, and requires no task-specific
training. Accordingly, TempoMem does not compete with long-term memory
models as a mechanism for increasing predictive context. It introduces
a distinct form of memory whose primary role is to reduce inference
cost.

\paragraph{Persistent semantic memory for efficient inference.}
The preceding lines of work address three related but distinct forms of
redundancy. Spatial reduction removes unnecessary tokens from the
current frame; short-term temporal methods reuse representations from
recent, usually position-aligned observations; and memory-augmented
models retain past information to improve predictive context. TempoMem
targets a fourth setting: \emph{long-term semantic recurrence}. It
maintains a bounded, content-addressable memory of scene representations
and reuses previously computed transformer operations when equivalent
visual content reappears, even after spatial displacement or an extended
temporal gap.

This distinction is loosely related to key--value caching in
autoregressive language models, where previously computed keys and
values are retained to avoid recomputing the prefix at every generation
step. Video inference, however, presents a different challenge: an
incoming visual token does not have a predetermined correspondence with
a particular past token. Efficient reuse therefore requires determining
which previously observed content is semantically equivalent, how it
should be compressed into bounded memory, and when approximation remains
safe for the downstream prediction task. TempoMem addresses these
questions through persistent prototype construction and content-based
retrieval.

\section{Persistent Semantic Memory for Efficient Inference} %Flavio: Methodology is too general
\label{sec:method}

It is known that transformer acceleration relies on caching. However, existing caches are fundamentally identity-based, i.e., cached representations are reused only when the same token, spatial location, or recent temporal context is encountered again. This naturally raises a broader question:
\emph{can transformer inference exploit content-addressable semantic
caching instead?} In particular, can previously computed internal
representations be indexed by the visual content they encode, rather than
by when or where they were computed?

We hypothesize that a substantial fraction of transformer computation is
associated with recurring scene content rather than individual frames.
Consequently, if semantically equivalent visual content induces
sufficiently similar internal representations, those representations can
be retrieved and reused in place of redundant computation, even after
camera motion or long temporal gaps.
%
TempoMem is built upon this hypothesis. Rather than caching
representations according to token identity or temporal locality, it
constructs a compact \emph{Persistent Scene Memory (PSM)} that is queried
by content. During inference, each transformer block summarizes recurring
visual representations into semantic prototypes and associates them with
their corresponding transformer activations. Incoming tokens retrieve
these cached representations through content similarity, allowing
previous computations to be reused whenever the memory sufficiently
explains the observed scene. Because the memory is independent of the
underlying model parameters, TempoMem requires no retraining and can
operate either as a standalone accelerator or as a lightweight layer on
top of existing temporal acceleration methods.


\subsection{Persistent Scene Memory}

The semantic caching hypothesis introduced requires a memory that can retrieve previously computed transformer representations based on content rather than token identity or temporal locality. TempoMem realizes this idea through a \emph{Persistent Scene Memory (PSM)}, a compact content-addressable memory that stores reusable semantic representations accumulated throughout
inference.

Unlike conventional caches, whose entries are indexed by position or
execution order, PSM organizes memory according to semantic similarity.
Consequently, visually similar scene content can retrieve previously
computed representations even when it reappears at different locations or
after long temporal gaps. To remain practical for long video sequences,
the memory must remain compact, support efficient retrieval, and evolve
online without requiring retraining.
%
We satisfy these requirements by representing PSM as a set of semantic
prototypes, each associated with the transformer representations it
encodes. The following subsection describes how these prototypes are
constructed and maintained during inference.

\subsection{Memory Construction}%Not prototype cache... 

PSM is constructed online during inference. Since no semantic memory is
available at the beginning of a video, TempoMem first observes a short
bootstrap segment to initialize the memory. Rather than storing every
token representation, PSM maintains a compact set of semantic prototypes
that summarize recurring scene content.
%
For each token representation $\mathbf{z}_i$ produced by the current
transformer block, TempoMem measures its similarity to the existing
prototypes: $s_j = \mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_j)$,
where $\mathbf{p}_j$ denotes the $j$-th prototype and
$\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is cosine similarity.
%
If the maximum similarity exceeds a threshold $\tau$, the corresponding
prototype is updated to incorporate the new observation; otherwise, a new
prototype is created. Consequently, the memory grows according to the
semantic diversity of the video rather than its duration.

Each prototype stores not only its semantic representation, but also the
transformer activations associated with it, enabling their subsequent
reuse during inference. The next subsection describes how incoming token
representations query PSM to retrieve previously computed activations.



\subsection{Memory Retrieval}

Once PSM has been initialized, every incoming token first queries the
memory before executing the standard transformer computations. Given its
semantic representation $\mathbf{z}_i$, TempoMem retrieves the most
similar prototype,
%
\begin{equation}
j^*=\arg\max_j \; \mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_j),
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{p}_j$ denotes the $j$-th semantic prototype.

The similarity score determines whether the cached representation
provides a reliable explanation of the current scene content. If
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{p}_{j^*}) \ge \tau,
\end{equation}
%
the retrieved prototype is considered semantically equivalent to the
incoming token and its associated transformer activations become
available for reuse. Otherwise, the token follows the standard inference
path, and its representation may subsequently be incorporated into PSM as
described in the previous subsection.

This retrieval mechanism extends conventional caching from exact
identity-based reuse to approximate semantic reuse, allowing previously
computed representations to remain useful even when recurring scene
content appears at different spatial locations or after long temporal
gaps. The next subsection describes how the retrieved activations are
integrated into the transformer computation.



\subsection{Representation Reuse}

Memory retrieval determines whether an incoming token is semantically
equivalent to previously observed scene content. When this condition is
satisfied, TempoMem reuses the transformer representations associated
with the retrieved prototype instead of recomputing them.
%
Concretely, the matched prototype replaces the corresponding semantic
representation, while its cached key and value projections are injected
directly into the attention computation. Only unmatched tokens require
fresh projection and attention computation, allowing the transformer to
preserve prediction quality while substantially reducing redundant
operations. Algorithm~\ref{alg:standalone} summarizes the complete inference procedure for a transformer block operating with TempoMem.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Semantic Memory Inference with TempoMem}
\label{alg:standalone}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Tokens $X$; phase; memory $\mathcal{M}$; buffer $B$;
background ratio $\beta$; reuse ratio $r$

\If{phase is \textsc{caching}}
    \Statex \Comment{\textbf{Build or refresh the semantic memory}}
    \State Run dense inference on $X$
    \State Add low-saliency background tokens and their activations to $B$
    \If{the caching interval ends}
        \State Compress $B$ into semantic prototypes and update $\mathcal{M}$
        \State Clear $B$
    \EndIf
    \State \Return dense output
\Else
    \Statex \Comment{\textbf{Reuse recurring content from memory}}
    \State Match background tokens to prototypes in $\mathcal{M}$
    \State Reuse prototypes and cached activations for the best $r$ matches
    \State Recompute foreground and unmatched tokens
    \State \Return the block output on the reduced sequence
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Layered Integration}

Although TempoMem can operate as a standalone acceleration mechanism, the
semantic caching principle is independent of the underlying inference
strategy. Rather than replacing existing efficient transformers,
TempoMem complements them by providing an additional source of semantic
reuse. Given any host acceleration method that selects a subset of tokens for recomputation, TempoMem first determines whether those tokens can be explained by its Persistent Scene Memory. Tokens that are successfully matched are substituted or skipped according to the host's execution model, while the remaining tokens follow the original inference path. Consequently, TempoMem augments existing acceleration techniques without modifying their optimization objectives or training procedures. Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered} summarizes this layered execution strategy.



\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Layered Semantic Memory Inference}%(one block, one frame)}
\label{alg:layered}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require
  Frame tokens; host method with its per-frame token selection;
  prototype cache $E$; buffer $B$; reuse fraction $\gamma$
  \vspace{0.3em}

\State $C \gets$ recompute set of $k$ tokens proposed by the host
  \hfill\Comment{Tokens the host flags as useful}
\If{phase is \textsc{caching}}
  \State Append the tokens of $C$ to $B$; on the last caching frame,
    merge $B$ with $T$ BSM passes into $E$
  \State \Return standard host output on $C$
\Else
  \State Compare each candidate to every prototype in $E$
    by cosine similarity
  \State $D \gets$ the $\lfloor \gamma k \rfloor$ candidates most similar
    to a prototype
    \hfill\Comment{Special tokens always protected}
  \State $R \gets C \setminus D$
    \hfill\Comment{Kept set: tokens the cache cannot explain}
  \If{substitution variant}
    \State Host recomputes all of $C$, with each input in $D$
      overwritten by its best-matching prototype
  \Else
    \State Host recomputes only $R$
  \EndIf
  \State Dropped tokens follow the host's existing reuse path
  \State \Return host output
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

TempoMem introduces an additional semantic retrieval step before each transformer block. Its computational benefit therefore depends on a simple trade-off: the cost of retrieving reusable representations from the Persistent Scene Memory versus the cost of recomputing those representations using the original transformer block.
%
We analyze the computational complexity of a single transformer block during inference and compare the proposed approach against \emph{dense inference}, i.e., the standard execution in which every input token is processed normally without any representation reuse. For an input containing $N$ tokens with embedding dimension $C$, the dominant floating-point operations (FLOPs) of dense inference are
approximately:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{dense}}
\approx
4NC^2
+
8NC^2
+
4N^2C,
\end{equation}

where the first term corresponds to the query, key, value, and output linear projections, the second term accounts for the two-layer feed-forward network, and the final quadratic term captures the self-attention computation.
 During a matching frame, TempoMem replaces a fraction of the background tokens with cached semantic representations, so only the
remaining unmatched tokens require fresh key/value computation. The
resulting cost becomes:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{TM}}
\approx
10N'C^2
+
2(N'-M_u)C^2
+
4N'^2C
+
N_cMC,
\end{equation}

where the final term is the semantic retrieval cost. Since $N'<N$, the
dominant attention computation is reduced quadratically, while all
pointwise operations scale with the reduced token count.

Moreover, semantic retrieval quickly amortizes its own overhead.
Matching requires $O(N_cM)$ similarity computations, whereas every
matched token avoids recomputing its key/value projections. Retrieval is
therefore beneficial whenever $M < 2rC$,
where $r$ is the reuse ratio. For a typical ViT embedding dimension
($C=768$) and $r=0.75$, the break-even point is $M<1152$ prototypes.
TempoMem typically operates with one to two orders of magnitude fewer
prototypes, so the retrieval cost is negligible compared to the
computation it eliminates.

Finally, unlike temporal window caches, whose memory grows linearly with
the number of stored frames, the Persistent Scene Memory has a fixed
capacity. Each transformer block stores $O(MC)$ values, yielding
$O(LMC)$ memory for an $L$-layer transformer, independent of the length
of the video stream.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Settings and Details}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and evaluation setup.}
We evaluate on video object detection using the ILSVRC 2015 ImageNet VID
dataset, reporting on the full 639-video validation split with mean average
precision at IoU $0.5$ (mAP@50), following the protocol of prior work
\cite{eventful,maskvd}. Ablations use a fixed $10\%$ subsample (64 videos)
drawn once and shared by every configuration, so all ablation comparisons
are paired. Compute is reported as GFLOPs measured with in-code operation
counters (instrumented linear, matmul, einsum, bias and add modules) rather
than analytic estimates, so every reported reduction reflects operations
actually executed, \emph{including} the cost of TempoMem's own matching.
Latency is CUDA-event wall-clock time per frame and memory is peak GPU
allocation, both measured on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 (24\,GB) under
a shared warm-up and reset protocol applied identically to every method.

\paragraph{Model design for video detection.}
Our detection model is ViTDet-B \cite{vitdet}, a plain ViT-B \cite{vit}
backbone with a standard detection head. The backbone has 12 blocks with
global self-attention at blocks 3, 6, 9 and 12 and $14 \times 14$ windowed
attention elsewhere; tokens are 768-dimensional and attention uses 12 heads
with learned relative position embeddings. We evaluate at $672 \times 672$
input resolution, which yields 1{,}764 tokens per frame. Because windowed
blocks carry relative-position biases, TempoMem runs in its KV-reuse-only
variant on those blocks and in its full form on the global-attention
blocks, which account for the bulk of the self-attention cost.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol and cost accounting.}
All weights are frozen, pretrained checkpoints; TempoMem and every compared
method are applied as training-free, inference-time modifications, so all
comparisons isolate the reuse mechanism itself.

\emph{Replay matching.} Each video is processed twice. The first $W = 4$
frames run in caching mode to build the persistent memory; this pass is
\textbf{discarded and uncounted} --- its predictions are thrown away and its
FLOPs excluded. The model then \textbf{replays the entire video from frame
0} in matching mode, and only this second pass is scored and counted.
mAP@50 therefore covers every frame of the video, and GFLOPs/frame is the
steady-state cost of a warm model, with no warm-up amortization credited to
the method. The dense, Eventful, STGT and MaskVD baselines are evaluated on
the same frames under the same accounting, so every number in this section
is directly comparable.

\emph{Layered instantiation.} Eventful Transformers \cite{eventful} are the
host throughout. An Eventful block gates the qkv input, the attention-output
projection and the MLP, selecting the top-$k$ most-changed tokens for
recomputation and reusing buffered values elsewhere; $k$ is its compute
budget. The layered filter plugs in at the qkv gate: it drops the fraction
$\gamma$ of the gate's top-$k$ proposal that best matches the persistent
memory and forces the surviving set onto all three gates
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered}). With $\gamma = 0$ every gate receives exactly
its Eventful proposal, so the pipeline \emph{is} Eventful, and every layered
comparison is controlled down to the single knob $\gamma$.

\subsection{Comparison with the State of the Art}
\label{sec:eval-sota}

\paragraph{Accuracy versus compute.}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places every compared method in the
accuracy--compute plane at a common token budget ($k = 512$ where
applicable). Layered TempoMem reaches the lowest compute cost in the
comparison by a clear margin, 46.6 GFLOPs/frame --- a $73\%$ reduction
against the dense backbone and $23\%$ below Eventful --- and is the only
method under $60$ GFLOPs. It also records the lowest accuracy, 79.87\%
mAP@50, against 81.80\% for Eventful, 82.05\% for MaskVD and 82.28\% for
dense. The $23\%$ compute saving over the host therefore costs $1.9$ mAP
points. Sweeping the host's budget confirms that this trade is not
favourable: Eventful configured to spend the \emph{same} compute as
TempoMem ($k = 384$, 47.1 GFLOPs) scores 81.40\% mAP@50, so at matched cost
the host alone is $1.5$ points more accurate. Spending a fixed budget on a
larger Eventful recompute set beats spending it on a smaller set plus
content-based reuse. We report this as measured rather than tuning around
it: on dense spatial prediction the recompute-set filter buys real compute
savings but does not advance the accuracy--compute frontier.

There is one configuration TempoMem does improve on. Eventful can itself be
compounded with a spatial-redundancy mechanism --- $2 \times 2$ pooling of
the attention keys and values --- and this variant is the closest published
analogue to a compounded accelerator. Against it, TempoMem is both cheaper
(46.6 vs.\ 52.8 GFLOPs/frame, $-12\%$) and marginally more accurate
(79.87\% vs.\ 79.48\%), so as a way of buying the last increment of compute
reduction on top of a temporal host, persistent memory is competitive with
spatial pooling. The advantage is narrow, and it does not extend to latency
or memory (below), but it delimits where the mechanism is worth using.

\paragraph{Deployment cost: latency and memory.}
Table~\ref{tab:vid672} reports the same comparison with measured wall-clock
latency and peak memory, which tell a different story from FLOPs alone.
Layered TempoMem converts the largest FLOP reduction in the table into the
\emph{smallest} relative speedup: at 60.0\,ms it is faster than the dense
backbone (70.1\,ms) but slower than every gated baseline, including STGT
(55.1\,ms), MaskVD (54.9\,ms) and the spatially pooled Eventful variant
(49.0\,ms), all of which execute more nominal FLOPs. The last of these is
the sharpest illustration: it costs $13\%$ more FLOPs than TempoMem yet
runs $18\%$ faster.
The cause is implementation-level rather than algorithmic --- the
cosine-similarity matching product and the gather/scatter steps are unfused
kernels --- but it means the theoretical saving does not yet reach the
deployment metric that matters. Memory moves against the method even more
clearly: at 2329\,MB TempoMem is the most memory-hungry configuration
measured, more than double the dense backbone, because it maintains the
persistent memory \emph{in addition to} the host's existing reference and
accumulator buffers rather than replacing them. Reducing that overhead
through buffer sharing, and recovering the latency through fused kernels,
are the two concrete engineering steps this evaluation identifies.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Reuse fraction $\gamma$ governs the accuracy--compute exchange.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates the reuse fraction $\gamma$, the single
knob that determines how much of the host's recompute set the persistent
memory absorbs. Compute savings grow smoothly and substantially with
$\gamma$, from $73.3\%$ to $95.2\%$ against the dense backbone as the
effective token-skipping ratio rises from $78.2\%$ to $98.5\%$. Accuracy,
however, does not degrade proportionally --- it degrades
\emph{super-linearly}. The first step is nearly free: at $\gamma = 0.25$ the
filter discards a quarter of the proposed tokens for $2.8$ mAP points,
already delivering almost three quarters of the achievable saving. Doubling
the reuse fraction to $\gamma = 0.5$ costs $7.3$ points for only $7.8$
further points of saving, and beyond that the exchange collapses: $\gamma =
0.75$ loses $18.6$ points and $\gamma = 0.95$ loses $44.0$, by which stage
the detector has been degraded past usefulness in exchange for a final
$6$ points of compute. The practical operating range for dense spatial
prediction is therefore the low-$\gamma$ regime, and $\gamma = 0.25$ is the
configuration we carry into the comparison above. A second knob, the number
of merge passes $T$ used to compress the memory into prototypes, proved
nearly inert on this task: across $T \in \{2,4,6,8\}$ accuracy varies by at
most $1.2$ mAP points at fixed $\gamma$, and $T$ does not change FLOPs at
all, since merging alters \emph{which} tokens the memory holds rather than
how many are recomputed.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented TempoMem, a training-free \emph{semantic caching mechanism} for efficient video transformer inference. Rather than reusing computation according to temporal locality, TempoMem maintains a compact Persistent Scene Memory that retrieves and reuses transformer representations associated with recurring scene content. This semantic memory can operate either as a standalone accelerator or as a lightweight layer on top of existing temporal acceleration methods.
%
Our experiments demonstrated that semantic caching reduces
computation while maintaining competitive predictive performance,
particularly for video classification tasks, and integrates naturally
with existing efficient transformer architectures. More broadly, we believe that persistent semantic memory provides a new
primitive for efficient transformer inference, complementing temporal
reuse and opening the door to memory-centric architectures for
long-running visual reasoning.
