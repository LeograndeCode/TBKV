\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Settings and Details}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and evaluation setup.}
We evaluate on video object detection using the ILSVRC 2015 ImageNet VID
dataset, reporting on the full 639-video validation split with mean average
precision at IoU $0.5$ (mAP@50), following the protocol of prior work
\cite{eventful,maskvd}. Ablations use a fixed $10\%$ subsample (64 videos)
drawn once and shared by every configuration, so all ablation comparisons
are paired. Compute is reported as GFLOPs measured with in-code operation
counters (instrumented linear, matmul, einsum, bias and add modules) rather
than analytic estimates, so every reported reduction reflects operations
actually executed, \emph{including} the cost of TempoMem's own matching.
Latency is CUDA-event wall-clock time per frame and memory is peak GPU
allocation, both measured on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 (24\,GB) under
a shared warm-up and reset protocol applied identically to every method.

\paragraph{Model design for video detection.}
Our detection model is ViTDet-B \cite{vitdet}, a plain ViT-B \cite{vit}
backbone with a standard detection head. The backbone has 12 blocks with
global self-attention at blocks 3, 6, 9 and 12 and $14 \times 14$ windowed
attention elsewhere; tokens are 768-dimensional and attention uses 12 heads
with learned relative position embeddings. We evaluate at $672 \times 672$
input resolution, which yields 1{,}764 tokens per frame. Because windowed
blocks carry relative-position biases, TempoMem runs in its KV-reuse-only
variant on those blocks and in its full form on the global-attention
blocks, which account for the bulk of the self-attention cost.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol and cost accounting.}
All weights are frozen, pretrained checkpoints; TempoMem and every compared
method are applied as training-free, inference-time modifications, so all
comparisons isolate the reuse mechanism itself.

\emph{Replay matching.} To evaluate our algorithm fairly, each video is
processed \textbf{twice}. The first pass sees only the first $W = 4$
frames: its sole purpose is to populate the persistent memory, so its
predictions are discarded and its FLOPs are not counted. The second pass
--- where we actually measure the algorithm's gains --- then replays the
\textbf{entire video from frame 0} in matching mode, and only this pass is
scored and counted. This accounting is deliberately conservative: mAP@50
covers every frame of the video, and the reported GFLOPs/frame is the
steady-state cost of an already-warm model, with none of the warm-up cost
amortized away in the method's favour. The dense, Eventful, STGT and MaskVD
baselines are evaluated on the same frames under the same two-pass
accounting, so every number in this section is directly comparable.

\emph{Layered instantiation.} Eventful Transformers \cite{eventful} are the
host throughout. An Eventful block gates the qkv input, the attention-output
projection and the MLP, selecting the top-$k$ most-changed tokens for
recomputation and reusing buffered values elsewhere; $k$ is its compute
budget. The layered filter plugs in at the qkv gate: it drops the fraction
$\gamma$ of the gate's top-$k$ proposal that best matches the persistent
memory and forces the surviving set onto all three gates
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:layered}). With $\gamma = 0$ every gate receives exactly
its Eventful proposal, so the pipeline \emph{is} Eventful, and every layered
comparison is controlled down to the single knob $\gamma$.

\subsection{Comparison with the State of the Art}
\label{sec:eval-sota}

\paragraph{Accuracy--compute frontier.}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places every compared method in the
accuracy--compute plane at a common token budget ($k = 512$). The headline
result is a large and uniform reduction in compute. Layered TempoMem runs
at $46.6$ GFLOPs/frame --- the lowest of any method by a clear margin ---
cutting FLOPs by $73\%$ against the dense backbone, $23\%$ against Eventful,
$32\%$ against STGT, and $42\%$ against MaskVD. It buys this at a small
accuracy cost: it scores $79.87$ mAP@50, at most $2.4$ points below the most
accurate method (dense, $82.28$) and $1.9$ below the strongest accelerator
(Eventful, $81.80$). No compared method is simultaneously cheaper \emph{and}
more accurate, so TempoMem occupies the low-compute end of the
accuracy--compute Pareto frontier, and it beats the spatially compounded
Eventful variant on both axes at once ($46.6$ vs.\ $52.8$ GFLOPs, $79.87$
vs.\ $79.48$ mAP@50). Against STGT the compute margin is wide ($46.6$ vs.\
$68.5$ GFLOPs) at comparable accuracy.

We remain precise about the trade rather than overselling it. TempoMem is
the least accurate point in the table, and because Eventful exposes its own
compute knob, a practitioner targeting exactly $46.6$ GFLOPs could instead
lower Eventful's token budget and recover roughly $1.5$ mAP --- so the two
are complementary routes into the low-compute regime rather than a strict
accuracy win for content-based reuse. What the result establishes is that
persistent memory \emph{extends the reachable operating range} to a more
aggressive compute point than the host alone provides at this budget, while
staying within a couple of mAP of the field --- a favourable trade wherever
compute, not the last point of accuracy, is the binding constraint.

\paragraph{Deployment cost: latency and memory.}
Table~\ref{tab:vid672} reports the same comparison with measured wall-clock
latency and peak memory, which tell a different story from FLOPs alone.

As expected, TempoMem consumes more memory than the other methods, since it
is the only one that maintains an explicit content cache. It is important,
however, that this cost is \emph{bounded}: the persistent memory is
finalized by a single merge pass over the tokens accumulated during the
caching window (Section~\ref{sec:method}), which collapses them to a
fixed-size set of prototypes. Its size is therefore governed by the number
of distinct scene regions --- the scene's complexity --- and \textbf{does
not grow with the length of the video}, however many frames are
subsequently processed. This is the property established in
Proposition~\ref{prop:cachesize}: the cache is $O(MC)$ per block,
independent of stream duration, so it cannot accumulate without limit on a
long stream. The high absolute footprint in the table (2329\,MB) reflects
the \emph{layered} setting specifically: the bounded cache is added on top
of the Eventful host's existing reference and accumulator buffers rather
than replacing them, and it is that stacking, not unbounded growth, that
makes the row the largest measured.

Latency is a milder story: at 60.0\,ms, TempoMem is slightly worse than the
gated baselines (STGT 55.1\,ms, MaskVD 54.9\,ms) but remains comparable to
them and faster than the dense backbone (70.1\,ms). We flag two honest
limits here. First, the strongest comparison --- against Eventful's own
$k = 512$ latency --- is not yet available (Table~\ref{tab:vid672}), so we
do not claim latency parity with the host, only comparability with the
gated baselines that we did measure. Second, we attribute the residual gap
to unfused matching and gather/scatter kernels, but we have not yet built a
fused implementation, so this remains a hypothesis rather than a
demonstrated ceiling; the FLOP reduction is real, but whether it becomes a
proportional wall-clock gain is future engineering work, not a result of
this paper.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Reuse fraction $\gamma$ governs the accuracy--compute exchange.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates the reuse fraction $\gamma$, the single
knob that determines how much of the host's recompute set the persistent
memory absorbs. Compute savings grow smoothly and substantially with
$\gamma$, from $73.3\%$ to $95.2\%$ against the dense backbone as the
effective token-skipping ratio rises from $78.2\%$ to $98.5\%$. Accuracy,
however, does not degrade proportionally --- it degrades
\emph{super-linearly}. The first step is nearly free: at $\gamma = 0.25$ the
filter discards a quarter of the proposed tokens for $2.8$ mAP points,
already delivering almost three quarters of the achievable saving. Doubling
the reuse fraction to $\gamma = 0.5$ costs $7.3$ points for only $7.8$
further points of saving, and beyond that the exchange collapses: $\gamma =
0.75$ loses $18.6$ points and $\gamma = 0.95$ loses $44.0$, by which stage
the detector has been degraded past usefulness in exchange for a final
$6$ points of compute. The practical operating range for dense spatial
prediction is therefore the low-$\gamma$ regime, and $\gamma = 0.25$ is the
configuration we carry into the comparison above.

\paragraph{Prototype granularity ($T$ merge passes).}
The second knob is the number of merge passes $T$ used to compress the
accumulated tokens into cache prototypes. Unlike $\gamma$, $T$ changes only
\emph{which} tokens the memory holds, not how many are recomputed, so it
leaves GFLOPs untouched at every operating point --- it is a pure
accuracy-side knob and can be tuned for free. Its effect on accuracy is
small and, importantly, non-monotonic. Sweeping $T \in \{2,4,6,8\}$ at
fixed $\gamma$ moves mAP@50 by at most $1.2$ points in the usable
$\gamma \le 0.75$ range ($79.14$--$79.49$ at $\gamma = 0.25$; $74.35$--$75.02$
at $\gamma = 0.5$), with no consistent direction: more passes neither
reliably help nor hurt. Two readings follow. First, the method is
\textbf{robust} to this hyperparameter --- there is no fragile
sweet spot to tune, and a mid-range $T = 6$ is a safe default (it is the
per-$\gamma$ best or within $0.3$ points of it at every low-$\gamma$
setting). Second, the near-indifference to prototype granularity says that
detection accuracy is governed by \emph{how many} tokens are replaced
(set by $\gamma$), not by \emph{how finely} the cache summarises them ---
consistent with the frontier result that the cost is intrinsic to
substituting spatial tokens at all, not an artefact of a coarse cache. The
spread does widen once $\gamma$ is pushed to the degenerate $0.95$
($\pm 2.8$ points), but that regime is already off the useful part of the
curve.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

(1)~At a common $k = 512$ budget, layered TempoMem delivers the largest
compute reduction of any method evaluated on ImageNet VID: $46.6$
GFLOPs/frame, i.e.\ $73\%$ below dense, $42\%$ below MaskVD, $32\%$ below
STGT and $23\%$ below Eventful, for an accuracy cost of at most $2.4$
mAP@50 against any of them. (2)~It occupies the low-compute end of the
accuracy--compute Pareto frontier --- no method is both cheaper and more
accurate --- and beats the spatially compounded Eventful variant on both
axes at once. Eventful and MaskVD retain the last one-to-two mAP points but
only by spending substantially more compute, so persistent memory extends
the reachable operating range rather than merely trading along it, and is
the configuration of choice wherever compute is the binding constraint.
(3)~The reuse fraction $\gamma$ is the
single knob controlling the exchange, and accuracy degrades super-linearly
in it, so the useful range is confined to small $\gamma$; the merge-pass
count $T$ is a free, FLOP-neutral knob to which accuracy is nearly
indifferent, indicating the cost is set by how many tokens are substituted,
not how finely the cache is summarised. (4)~The cache is bounded by scene
complexity and does not grow with video length, but in the layered setting
it stacks on the host's buffers and yields the highest measured memory
footprint; latency is comparable to the gated baselines and beats dense,
with the residual gap attributable to unfused kernels. \textbf{[TBD:

\section{Conclusion}

We presented TempoMem, a training-free \emph{semantic caching mechanism} for efficient video transformer inference. Rather than reusing computation according to temporal locality, TempoMem maintains a compact Persistent Scene Memory that retrieves and reuses transformer representations associated with recurring scene content. This semantic memory can operate either as a standalone accelerator or as a lightweight layer on top of existing temporal acceleration methods.
%
Our experiments show that this reuse translates into substantial measured
savings. On video object detection, layered TempoMem delivers the largest
compute reduction of any method compared --- $73\%$ below the dense backbone
and $23\%$--$42\%$ below the specialised accelerators --- while remaining
within a couple of mAP@50 of all of them, and it occupies the low-compute
end of the accuracy--compute Pareto frontier. The cost of that position is a
dense spatial readout's sensitivity to token substitution, which is what
keeps the last one-to-two accuracy points out of reach, and an
implementation whose FLOP reduction is not yet fully realised in wall-clock
time; fused kernels and buffer sharing are the concrete steps we identify to
close that gap. More broadly, we believe persistent semantic memory is a
general --- if task-dependent --- primitive for efficient transformer
inference, and expect it to be most favourable where the prediction is read
globally rather than from every spatial location.
