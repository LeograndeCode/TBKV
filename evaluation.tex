\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Settings and Details}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets and metrics.}
Our primary benchmark is video object detection on the ILSVRC 2015
ImageNet VID dataset: dense spatial prediction is the hardest case for
token substitution and therefore the most informative stress test of
persistent reuse. We report mean average precision at IoU $0.5$ (mAP@50)
on the full 639-video validation split, following the protocol of prior
work \cite{eventful,maskvd}. Two companion evaluations broaden the
scope: video action recognition with ViViT on Kinetics-400, where the
prediction is read globally from a class token rather than per location
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-vivit}), and the same detection benchmark at
$1024 \times 1024$ input resolution, which raises the frame from 1{,}764
to 4{,}096 tokens and so tests how persistent reuse behaves when the
recompute budget is held fixed against a growing frame. Compute
is reported in GFLOPs measured with in-code operation counters rather
than analytic estimates: every reported reduction reflects operations
actually executed, including the cost of PSM's own matching. Latency is
CUDA-event wall-clock time per frame and memory is peak GPU allocation,
both measured on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 (24\,GB) under a
warm-up and reset protocol applied identically to every method.

\paragraph{Ablation split and hyperparameter selection.}
Ablations use a fixed $10\%$ subsample of each dataset (64 of the 639
VID videos, 1{,}988 of the 19{,}877 Kinetics-400 clips), drawn once and
shared by every configuration, so all ablation comparisons are paired;
the dense reference for every reported delta is recomputed on the same
subsample. The subsamples come from the validation splits, as in prior
work \cite{eventful,maskvd}: neither benchmark provides a third
partition for tuning under the standard protocol. The operating points
carried into the full-split comparisons ($\gamma = 0.25$, $T = 6$ for
detection; $\gamma = 0.95$, $T = 2$ for action recognition) were fixed
on the subsamples before the full-split runs. Single-run differences below roughly one
point (mAP@50 on VID, Top-1 on Kinetics-400) on these subsamples should
be read as within noise.

\paragraph{Detection model.}
The detector is ViTDet-B \cite{vitdet}, a plain ViT-B backbone \cite{vit}
with a standard detection head: 12 blocks, with global self-attention at
blocks 3, 6, 9 and 12 and $14 \times 14$ windowed attention elsewhere;
768-dimensional tokens, 12 heads, learned relative position embeddings.
The primary resolution is $672 \times 672$, which gives 1{,}764 tokens
per frame; the companion evaluation repeats the comparison at
$1024 \times 1024$, which gives 4{,}096. Every gated accelerator in the
detection evaluation (Eventful, STGT and layered PSM) runs at a single
common token budget of $k = 512$ at both resolutions, roughly $29\%$ of
the tokens at $672$ and $12.5\%$ at $1024$. We fix the budget once:
$k = 512$ is the operating point of the host's published sweep at which
accuracy is nearly saturated, so it is the strongest host to layer on,
and holding $k$ constant compares reuse mechanisms at equal budget
rather than budget choices. Carrying the same absolute $k$ across
resolutions means the $1024$ comparison runs at a substantially tighter
relative budget; that is the intent, since it isolates how a
fixed-size recompute set scales against a frame that more than doubles
in token count.
Windowed blocks carry relative-position biases, so PSM runs in its
KV-reuse-only variant there and in its full form on the global-attention
blocks, which account for most of the self-attention cost.

\paragraph{Protocol and cost accounting.}
All weights are frozen, pretrained checkpoints; PSM and every compared
method are applied as training-free, inference-time modifications, so
the comparison isolates the reuse mechanism itself.

\emph{Replay matching (detection).} Each VID video is processed twice.
The first pass sees only the first $W = 4$ frames and populates the
persistent memory; its predictions are discarded and its FLOPs are not
counted. The second pass replays the entire video from frame 0 in
matching mode, and only this pass is scored and costed. This accounting
is conservative in the direction that hurts our method: mAP@50 covers
every frame, and the reported GFLOPs per frame is the steady-state cost
of an already-warm model. All baselines are evaluated on the same frames
under the same two-pass accounting. The protocol models deployments in
which content recurs after the memory is built (archived or looped
footage, re-analysis of stored streams, stationary cameras); it does
not model a strictly single-pass live stream over never-repeated
content, and we make no claims for that setting. The action-recognition
benchmark adopts the complementary accounting, a single pass in which
warm-up is paid in full and counted; between them the two protocols
bracket the deployment space.

\emph{Layered instantiation.} Eventful Transformers \cite{eventful}
serve as the host throughout. An Eventful block gates the qkv input, the
attention-output projection and the MLP: it selects the top-$k$
most-changed tokens for recomputation and reuses buffered values for the
rest. The layered filter plugs in at the qkv gate. It removes the
fraction $\gamma$ of the gate's top-$k$ proposal that best matches the
persistent memory and forces the surviving set onto all three gates
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:eventfultm}). At $\gamma = 0$ the pipeline reduces
to Eventful itself, so every layered comparison is controlled down to
the single knob $\gamma$.

\subsection{Comparison with the State of the Art}
\label{sec:eval-sota}

\paragraph{Accuracy--compute trade at a common budget.}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places all methods in the accuracy--compute
plane at $672 \times 672$ and the common token budget. The main result is a large and uniform
reduction in compute. Layered PSM runs at $46.6$ GFLOPs per frame, the
lowest of any method by a clear margin: $73\%$ below the dense backbone,
$23\%$ below Eventful, $32\%$ below STGT and $42\%$ below MaskVD. The
accuracy cost is small: PSM scores $79.87$ mAP@50, at most $2.4$ points
below the most accurate method (dense, at $82.28$) and $1.9$ below the
strongest accelerator (Eventful, at $81.80$). No compared configuration
is simultaneously cheaper and more accurate, so PSM sits at the
low-compute end of the observed frontier, and it beats the spatially
compounded Eventful variant on both axes at once ($46.6$ vs.\ $52.8$
GFLOPs, $79.87$ vs.\ $79.48$ mAP@50); against STGT the compute margin is
wide at comparable accuracy.

This is a statement about the compared configurations at $k = 512$, not
about the full frontier each baseline could trace by sweeping its own
budget. In particular, interpolating Eventful's published
budget--accuracy curve, a configuration matched to $46.6$ GFLOPs would
recover roughly $1.5$ mAP over PSM; we plot this matched-FLOPs point in
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}. The two
mechanisms are therefore complementary routes into the low-compute
regime, not a strict accuracy win for content-based reuse: layered on
the host at a fixed budget, persistent memory reaches a more aggressive
compute point than the host alone provides while staying within a couple
of mAP points of the field, a trade that is attractive wherever compute,
not the last point of accuracy, is the binding constraint.

\paragraph{Deployment cost: latency and memory.}
The upper block of Table~\ref{tab:vid} reports the same comparison with
measured wall-clock latency and peak memory, which tell a different
story from FLOPs alone.

PSM has the highest memory footprint in the table, as expected: it is
the only method that maintains an explicit content cache. The breakdown
is informative, however. The host's own reference and accumulator
buffers already account for 2324\,MB, so the cache itself, collapsed to
a fixed-size set of prototypes at the end of the caching window, adds
only about 5\,MB on top; its size is governed by scene complexity and
does not grow with video length. The footprint is thus a property of the
layered setting rather than of the persistent memory, and buffer sharing
between cache and host is the direct route to reducing it.

Latency is a milder story. At 60.0\,ms, PSM runs within about a
millisecond of its host (Eventful, 58.9\,ms), slightly behind the other
gated baselines (STGT at 55.1\,ms, MaskVD at 54.9\,ms) and well ahead of
the dense backbone (70.1\,ms). We attribute the gap between the $23\%$
FLOP reduction over the host and this near-parity in wall-clock time to
unfused matching and gather/scatter kernels, but we have not built a
fused implementation, so this attribution is a hypothesis rather than a
demonstrated ceiling. The FLOP reduction is real; converting it into a
proportional wall-clock gain is engineering work that lies outside this
paper.

\paragraph{Scaling to $1024 \times 1024$.}
The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:vid} repeats the comparison at
$1024 \times 1024$, where a frame carries 4{,}096 tokens and the common
budget is therefore $12.5\%$ of them rather than $29\%$. Two things
change, one in each direction.

The compute advantage widens. PSM runs at $69.1$ GFLOPs per frame,
$85\%$ below the dense backbone against $73\%$ at $672$, and $58\%$
below STGT. This is the expected consequence of gating at a fixed
budget: the cost of a recompute set is set by $k$, not by the size of
the frame it is drawn from, so the saving relative to dense grows with
resolution. The same mechanism lifts Eventful with spatial pooling from
$30\%$ to $15\%$ of dense, while STGT improves only marginally
($39\%$ to $35\%$) because it gates the projections but still evaluates
attention over all tokens.

The accuracy picture tightens against us. Of the three methods measured
at both resolutions PSM loses the most, $4.3$ points
($79.87 \rightarrow 75.57$) against $3.8$ for STGT and $2.5$ for the
pooled variant, while the dense backbone gains $0.65$
($82.28 \rightarrow 82.93$). At $75.57$ mAP@50 PSM is $7.4$ points below
dense and, more tellingly, $1.4$ points below Eventful compounded with
spatial pooling at essentially the same compute ($69.1$ vs $70.9$
GFLOPs). The advantage over that compounded baseline observed at $672$,
where PSM won on both axes at once, therefore does not survive the
resolution change: at $1024$ the two sit at one compute point and the
pooled variant is the more accurate of them. PSM is still the cheapest
configuration measured here, but it buys the last $2.6\%$ of compute
with $1.4$ mAP, a worse exchange than the one it offers at $672$. We
read this as the dense-readout limit reasserting itself: at a $12.5\%$
budget the filter is substituting tokens the detection head reads
directly, and the reuse fraction that was near-free at $672$ is not at
$1024$. Re-tuning $\gamma$ per resolution, rather than carrying the
$672$ operating point over unchanged, is the obvious next step.

Memory is the other cost that grows. At $8062$\,MB PSM is the heaviest
method in the table by a factor of $2.4$ over the next-highest, and the
$672$ decomposition that attributed almost all of the footprint to the
host's own buffers cannot be repeated here, because the Eventful
reference at this resolution has not been measured. We report the number
without attributing it. Latency follows the $672$ pattern: at
$143.0$\,ms PSM is $19\%$ faster than dense but slower than the other
gated methods, consistent with the unfused matching and gather/scatter
kernels identified above.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Reuse fraction $\gamma$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates, on the detection benchmark, the reuse
fraction $\gamma$, the single knob that determines how much of the
host's recompute set the persistent memory absorbs; all deltas are
paired against the dense reference on the same $10\%$ subsample. Compute
savings grow smoothly with $\gamma$, from $73.3\%$ to $95.2\%$ relative
to the dense backbone, as the effective token-skipping ratio rises from
$78.2\%$ to $98.5\%$. Accuracy, however, degrades super-linearly rather
than in proportion. The first step is nearly free: at $\gamma = 0.25$
the filter discards a quarter of the proposed tokens for a cost of $2.8$
mAP points, and this already delivers almost three quarters of the
achievable saving. Doubling the reuse fraction to $\gamma = 0.5$ costs
$7.3$ points for only $7.8$ further points of saving. Beyond that the
exchange collapses: $\gamma = 0.75$ loses $18.6$ points and
$\gamma = 0.95$ loses $44.0$. These losses are an order of magnitude
above the subsample's noise floor, so the shape of the curve is not in
question. The practical operating range for dense
spatial prediction is therefore the low-$\gamma$ regime, and
$\gamma = 0.25$ is the configuration carried into the comparison above.

\paragraph{Prototype granularity ($T$ merge passes).}
The second knob is the number of merge passes $T$ used to compress the
accumulated tokens into cache prototypes. Unlike $\gamma$, $T$ changes
only which tokens the memory holds, not how many are recomputed, so it
leaves GFLOPs untouched at every operating point. Its effect on accuracy
is small and non-monotonic: sweeping $T \in \{2,4,6,8\}$ at fixed
$\gamma$ moves mAP@50 by at most $1.2$ points in the usable range
$\gamma \le 0.75$, with no consistent direction, and these spreads sit
near the subsample's single-run noise floor, so we do not interpret
their ordering. The supported conclusion is an insensitivity result:
there is no fragile sweet spot, and the mid-range value $T = 6$ is a
safe default, being the per-$\gamma$ best or within $0.3$ points of it
at every low-$\gamma$ setting. Moreover, detection accuracy is governed
by how many tokens are replaced, set by $\gamma$, not by how finely the
cache summarises them: the accuracy cost is intrinsic to substituting
spatial tokens at all, not an artefact of a coarse cache. The spread
widens only at the degenerate value $\gamma = 0.95$ ($\pm 2.8$ points),
already off the useful part of the curve.

\subsection{Video Action Recognition}
\label{sec:eval-vivit}

\paragraph{Task and model.}
The second benchmark moves to the opposite end of the readout spectrum:
video action recognition on Kinetics-400 \cite{k400}, which assigns one
of 400 action classes to a short ($\sim$10\,s) video clip. Where VID
scores a prediction at every spatial location, here the entire clip is
summarised into a single classification read from a class token, so this
task probes how token substitution behaves when the output is global
rather than dense. We report Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy on the full
19{,}877-clip validation split.

The model is ViViT-B \cite{vivit} in its factorised form, in the
configuration of the Eventful host \cite{eventful}: a ViT-B spatial
encoder (12 blocks, 768-dimensional tokens, 12 heads, a prepended class
embedding token, no windowed attention or relative position embeddings)
is applied sequentially to the sixteen two-frame tubelet steps of a
32-frame view, and a four-block temporal sub-model classifies the
resulting sequence of per-step embeddings. Predictions are averaged over
12 views per clip (4 temporal $\times$ 3 spatial crops) at
$224 \times 224$ resolution, which gives 197 tokens per step. The
spatial sub-model dominates the compute and is the component the host
accelerates. We evaluate the three budgets of the host's published
sweep, $k \in \{24, 48, 96\}$ of the 197 tokens per step; each budget
loads the host's published checkpoint, whose temporal sub-model was
fine-tuned on Eventful spatial outputs at that budget \cite{eventful}.
PSM is layered on top of these checkpoints with no further training,
exactly as on detection.

\paragraph{Single-pass protocol.}
This evaluation deliberately drops replay matching. Each clip is
processed exactly once, in temporal order: the first four temporal steps
of each view run through the host in caching mode and populate the
persistent memory, the remaining twelve run in matching mode, and every
operation of both phases is counted. Nothing is amortised away and no
frame is seen twice: the model starts cold on a stream, pays for its own
warm-up, and has to recover that investment within the footage it
actually sees. This is the mirror image of the replay protocol used for
detection, which isolates the steady state of an already-warm model;
reporting both regimes brackets the cost accounting rather than choosing
the flattering end of it.

\paragraph{Aggressive reuse is free on a global readout.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400-ablation} repeats the reuse-fraction ablation on the
Kinetics-400 $10\%$ subsample ($k = 24$). The contrast with detection is
stark. On VID, accuracy degraded super-linearly in $\gamma$; here it is
a step function. Accuracy drops once, when the filter first engages, and
is then flat to within $0.1$ Top-1 points across the entire sweep
($58.75\% \rightarrow 58.70\%$ from $\gamma = 0.25$ to $\gamma = 0.95$)
while the matching cost falls by $12\times$ ($329 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs
per clip). Sweeping the merge-pass count $T$ at fixed $\gamma$ moved
Top-1 by less than the reporting precision, so prototype granularity is
again a second-order effect; we carry $T = 2$
forward. The mechanism is the readout: a class token aggregates over all
spatial tokens, so the perturbation introduced by substituting
mostly-static background tokens is largely averaged out before it
reaches the classification, whereas a dense detection head reads every
substituted location directly. The persistent memory can therefore be
pushed far harder here than on detection: the selected operating point
is $\gamma = 0.95$, against $\gamma = 0.25$ on VID. The full-split runs
confirm that the step stays flat at scale: $\gamma = 0.5$ and
$\gamma = 0.95$ score $59.85\%$ and $59.82\%$ Top-1, respectively.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:k400} reports the full-split comparison across host
budgets at the selected operating point. At $k = 24$, layering PSM on
the host cuts the whole-clip cost by $39\%$ ($1053 \rightarrow 645$
GFLOPs) for $2.6$ Top-1 points ($62.38\% \rightarrow 59.82\%$), and cuts
the matching phase, the entire marginal cost of processing further
footage once the memory exists, by $16\times$
($435 \rightarrow 27$ GFLOPs). The favourable operating point is the
small budget: at $k = 48$ the one-time accuracy step is larger
($67.54\% \rightarrow 58.37\%$), so the filtered model surrenders the
accuracy the extra budget had bought.
We also note an honest quirk of single-pass accounting: at $k = 96$ the
host's own whole-clip cost ($3525$ GFLOPs) exceeds the dense backbone
($3360$), because on a 16-step clip the savings from gating do not
recover the cost of building and maintaining its reference buffers. The
matching column is the number that survives on longer footage.

\paragraph{The long-video reading.}
The split of Table~\ref{tab:k400} into caching and matching terms is the
point of the single-pass protocol. The caching term is paid once per
memory build, and in a 16-step clip it is amortised over only twelve
matched steps, the least favourable case this accounting can present. On
long footage, where the memory persists far beyond its caching window,
the amortised cost tends to
the matching column alone: at $k = 24$, $\gamma = 0.95$, that is $27$
GFLOPs per 16-step clip-equivalent, or about $2.3$ GFLOPs per temporal
step with all 12 views included, $94\%$ below the host's own matching
cost and roughly $1\%$ of the dense backbone. The scope of this
extrapolation is stated plainly: the measured clips are ten seconds
long, the current implementation freezes the memory after the caching
window, and accuracy under long-horizon content drift is unmeasured, so
the matching column is a ceiling on streaming savings rather than a
demonstrated long-stream result. What makes it credible is that the
per-clip accuracy cost of reaching it is the $2.6$-point step already
measured at full scale.

\subsection{Summary and Scope of Findings}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

On VID at the common budget, layered PSM is the cheapest compared method
($46.6$ GFLOPs per frame, $73\%$ below dense) for an accuracy cost of at
most $2.4$ mAP@50; no compared configuration is both cheaper and more
accurate, though a budget-matched Eventful point recovers roughly $1.5$
mAP, so the two mechanisms are complementary rather than substitutes.
Raising the input to $1024 \times 1024$ at the same absolute budget
sharpens both sides of that trade: the compute margin widens to $85\%$
below dense, but accuracy falls to $75.57$ mAP@50 and PSM no longer
dominates the spatially pooled Eventful variant, which is $1.4$ points
better at the same compute. The $672$ operating point does not transfer
unchanged to a $12.5\%$ token budget.
Accuracy is governed almost entirely by the reuse fraction $\gamma$,
degrading super-linearly under the dense detection readout, while the
merge-pass count $T$ is FLOP-neutral and nearly irrelevant. Action
recognition inverts this picture: under a global class-token readout
accuracy is a step function of $\gamma$, so the memory can absorb
$\gamma = 0.95$ of the recompute proposal, cutting whole-clip cost by
$39\%$ and matching cost by $16\times$ for $2.6$ Top-1 points under
fully counted single-pass accounting. Persistent reuse is thus most
tailored to global-readout tasks. The most promising extension is
streaming: on long footage the amortised cost tends to the matching term
alone, roughly $1\%$ of dense, and since matching already computes
token-to-prototype similarity, an additive cache update (folding matched
tokens into their prototypes, appending unexplained ones) would keep the
frozen memory current at negligible cost; we leave this to future work.

The scope of these claims: detection is compared at a single common
budget, carried unchanged across two input resolutions, under a replay
protocol that models recurring-content deployments; action recognition
uses single-pass accounting at three host budgets; and operating points
were selected on $10\%$ subsamples of the reported splits at
$672 \times 672$, as is standard for these benchmarks.
